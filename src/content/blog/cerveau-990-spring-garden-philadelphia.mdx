There is a cartoon boy painted across the arched window at 990 Spring Garden Street whose entire head has been replaced by a pepperoni pizza. He looks delighted about it. Harry Hayman went in, and the joke on the window turns out to explain the name, the menu and most of the building's recent history.

## The name, and the joke inside it

[Cerveau](https://cerveauphilly.com/) is French for brain. The chalkboard outside spells out the pronunciation for anybody who needs it, in green and red capitals: serve oh.

The reason it is called that is the head on the window. The restaurant [opened in November 2025](https://6abc.com/post/new-italian-restaurant-cerveau-opens-spring-garden-street/18448757/), and behind it is Joe Hunter, who founded Pizza Brain in Kensington. Pizza Brain was, among other things, a pizza shop that ran a museum of pizza memorabilia, and it occupied a particular place in this city's affections for well over a decade.

So the name is a translation of the old name, the window graphic is the old mascot, and the new place is the grown up version of the same idea. That is a rare thing to get right. Most second acts either pretend the first one did not happen or lean on it so hard that the new place never becomes anything.

## What Harry Hayman ate

The menu is Italian and deliberately unfussy, printed on paper with the same psychedelic lettering as the sign.

Small plates run from crispy mozzarella with marinara at eleven dollars to a single meatball described as un poco gordo at sixteen, charred broccolini with almond tahina at thirteen, and whipped ricotta in a sweet version with honey and pumpkin seeds or a spicy one with hot honey and crumbled nduja. Pizzas are sourdough crust and go from a plain jane at seventeen to a crab rangoon at twenty eight, by way of one called Queenie with three kinds of mushroom and one called Felix built on caramelised onion and gruyere. There are [tramezzini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tramezzino) style sandwiches, a smash burger, an eggplant parm, and a short pasta list where the cacio e pepe is fifteen and the rigatoni alla vodka is eighteen.

Harry Hayman had the crispy mozzarella and a glass of red, and the photographs show what arrived: two breaded batons on a checked paper liner with grated cheese over the top and a steel cup of marinara, next to a menu and a glass being filled from a bottle at the bar.

The bar has ambitions beyond the wine. The owner has said publicly that he would like the place to be known as Philadelphia's premier [spritz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spritz_Veneziano) bar, and the list runs cocktails with and without alcohol, which is now a requirement rather than a courtesy.

## The walls

The interior is the part that will make people photograph it, and it is worth describing properly because it is not decoration.

Every wall is painted, floor to ceiling, in a flat hard outlined cartoon style with a limited palette of orange, lilac, powder blue and mustard. A purple chef in a toque winks while holding a key on a chain, next to a giant fork skewering sausages and a tomato. A grey ogre with a purple mohawk wears a slice of pizza over one eye. A hand pours from a cocktail shaker across the ceiling line. There are clocks with arrows for hands, a paper aeroplane, a corkscrew, party hats.

None of it is Italian in any traditional sense and all of it is coherent, which is harder than it sounds. A restaurant that commits to a single artist and lets them cover every surface ends up with a room that cannot be replicated by anybody else, which is the opposite of what most new restaurants achieve.

## The building it is in

The address is the same one on the brass and steel plaque a few metres away on the same facade, and that plaque tells the longer story.

990 Spring Garden was designed by Rankin and Kellog and built in 1927 as the warehouse for the Philadelphia Wholesale Drug Company. It became the Packard Press printing works in the 1950s, stayed that way until the 1980s, and then sat vacant for decades before being reworked in 2016 by [Arts and Crafts Holdings](https://www.artsandcrafts.holdings/about) into a building of studios, makers and, now, restaurants.

Cerveau is not the only one. [The Philadelphia Inquirer reported](https://www.inquirer.com/food/restaurants/990-spring-garden-cerveau-lovechild-darnels-cakes-20250806.html) on a trio of new places arriving at the address together, which is what a converted warehouse looks like when the conversion actually works: not one anchor tenant, but several small operators who each need less space than the building has.

## Why the sequence matters

It is worth laying the dates next to each other, because they describe a mechanism rather than a mood.

A building goes up in 1927 for one industry. It changes industries in the 1950s. It is abandoned in the 1980s and stays empty for roughly thirty years. It is bought and reworked in 2016. A restaurant opens in it in late 2025, run by somebody whose previous restaurant was two miles away in a different neighbourhood, carrying a mascot and a joke forward from that one.

Almost a century, and the useful observation is that none of those steps was inevitable. The building could have come down at several points. The warehouse could have been converted into apartments with no ground floor life at all. The restaurant could have opened somewhere with better foot traffic and less risk.

Harry Hayman's argument about neighbourhoods is that they are made of exactly these decisions, taken one at a time by people who mostly cannot see the whole sequence they are part of.

## What a second restaurant costs its owner

The part of this that deserves more attention than it gets is how hard the move actually is.

Somebody who has run one successful restaurant for a decade has, on paper, every advantage opening a second one. They know the suppliers. They know what a bad month looks like. They have staff who would follow them and customers who would travel. What they do not have is any guarantee that the thing people loved about the first place survives the journey.

Plenty of beloved restaurants have opened a bigger, more ambitious sibling and watched it fail while the original kept humming, because the original was never really about the food. It was about a specific room, a specific street, a specific set of regulars and a scale small enough that the owner was in it every day. Reproduce the menu at four times the size in a different neighbourhood and the thing that made it work is the one ingredient that did not come along.

The way Cerveau seems to be handling that is by not pretending to be the same thing. The mascot travels and the joke travels, and everything else is different: Italian rather than pizza only, a full bar rather than a counter, a large painted room rather than a small one with a museum in it. That reads as a deliberate decision rather than a compromise.

Whether it works is decided over years, not on any one visit. But the structure of the bet is sound, and that is worth saying while it is still early enough to matter.

## The unremarkable recommendation

Go for the pizza and stay for the walls, or the other way round.

Spring Garden Street is not somewhere most Philadelphians think of going for dinner, which is precisely why the places opening there need people to turn up in the first year rather than the third. A restaurant with a decade of goodwill behind its owner still has to fill tables on a Tuesday in a neighbourhood people are only starting to remember exists.

There is a boy with a pizza for a head in the window pointing this out. He has been making the same argument in this city, in one form or another, for more than ten years.
