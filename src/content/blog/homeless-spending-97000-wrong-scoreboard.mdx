$97,000 a year per homeless person, and the man hearing that number remembers about $50 in burgers and hot dogs in the park. That gets my attention. In [Kevin Dahlgren's reel, posted November 12, 2026](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DfpYaRnhi_b/), the distance between a huge spending figure and the help a man can actually name is painful. Of course he wants answers. So do I. But telling him that $97,000 was spent on his care doesn't answer him. The math is real. The meaning is not. We've taken a regional spending total, divided it by a single night's count, and made it sound like somebody's personal receipt. I want to follow the money all the way to a home, and then find out whether the person kept it.

## The frustration deserves a hearing

Yes, $724 million is an enormous amount of money. Anyone hearing that figure while living on the street has every reason to ask where the help is. A report doesn't keep somebody warm. The man in the reel deserves to be heard, and Dahlgren's question deserves an answer: what did all that spending buy?

The reel names no city or year. Put those back in the sentence. A regional spending total without its location and dates can sound like the going price everywhere, for everybody. That's how a number becomes an argument before anyone has checked what it measures.

And Portland's count DID rise. [Portland State University's account of the regional count](https://www.pdx.edu/news/psu-homelessness-research-and-action-collaborative-releases-2025-tri-county-point-time-count) reports 12,034 people on January 22, 2025, up 61% from 2023. A new data set included for Multnomah County drove part of that increase, according to the release. Keep the increase and the counting change together. We need to understand both to make a useful comparison.

I hear the objection already: are we going to argue about the denominator while somebody needs a place to sleep? No. I'm arguing about it because the answer should help us decide what gets that person housed. His frustration deserves better than a figure that can't tell us what reached him.

## What the division actually divides

The spending estimate comes from [ECOnorthwest's Portland region report for fiscal 2024](https://econw.com/project/sources-and-uses-of-resources-to-address-homelessness-in-the-portland-region-3/). It puts spending across Clackamas, Multnomah and Washington counties at an estimated $724 million, covering July 2023 through June 2024. That's the money side of the calculation: a region's spending over a fiscal year.

In [Dahlgren's July 22, 2025 Substack explanation](https://truthonthestreets.substack.com/p/homeless-inc-the-most-profitable), he divides $724 million by 7,470 and gets just over $97,000. He also writes that the figure is not earmarked for every individual. Good. That qualification belongs right beside the number, where people will actually see it.

The [regional Point in Time count report](https://hsd.multco.us/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/2025-Tri-County-PITC-Report-11.04.25.pdf) gives the January 2023 count as 7,482. Divide $724 million by that and the result is about $96,800. Close to the viral figure. This isn't an argument about whether somebody can do division. It's about what that division tells us.

Now use the 2025 count of 12,034. Same spending, about $60,000 per counted person. Suddenly the number looks much smaller. Did anybody save money? No. Only the denominator changed, and that later count is paired with an earlier spending period. Neither ratio gives us a price for an individual's care or a measure of efficiency.

A year's activity and a single night's population are different things. Show me both, with the dates attached. Then show me what happened to the people the money was supposed to help. That's where the budget conversation starts getting useful.

## People who got homes leave the count

Here's the part that really bothers me about this scoreboard. Get somebody into a home and that person leaves the homeless count. The spending that helped them get there can still be in the total. We've built a ratio that loses sight of people at the very moment something good happens to them.

[Multnomah County's report for fiscal 2023 to 2024](https://multco.us/news/news-release-joint-office-homeless-services-reports-5500-people-re-housed-7900-people) says nearly 5,500 people moved into homes and left homelessness, and nearly 7,900 entered shelter. These are county figures, not results for the entire region, and the groups shouldn't be added together as distinct people. But they make the missing piece visible: people served throughout a year aren't the same population as people counted homeless on a particular night.

I've spent my working life in hospitality and business, where you judge a kitchen by what goes out the pass, not by the food budget divided by the number of chairs. I want that same attention to results here: who got a home, and did they keep it?

Entering shelter matters. Moving into a home matters. Those are different outcomes, and a placement alone doesn't tell me the housing lasted. I want the follow through, including the people who lost housing again. Otherwise we stop checking just when we need to learn whether the help held.

ECOnorthwest itself calls for better measures of cost per outcome. The people who added up the money are asking us to improve the scoreboard! So put the spending beside a clearly defined result. Explain which costs belong to it and how continued housing was checked. If an answer is unknown, mark it unknown. Then make finding it part of the work.

## Houston counts the progress and the setback

I want something we can learn from, and Houston gives me a place to start. [The Coalition for the Homeless of Houston/Harris County's regional results](https://www.cfthhouston.org/2024-pit-count-results) report more than 32,000 people placed in permanent housing through The Way Home since 2012, using Permanent Supportive Housing or Rapid Re-Housing. The same release reports a reduction in homelessness of more than 60% since 2011.

That's a regional system counting people placed in permanent housing and tracking homelessness over time. GREAT. Now we have something concrete to examine. How does the housing work connect to the count? What can Philadelphia learn from that regional approach? Those figures don't give us a cost per lasting housing outcome, but they give us a much better place to direct the next question.

And Houston keeps counting when the news gets harder. In its [2025 count results](https://www.cfthhouston.org/2025-pit-results), the coalition counted 3,325 people across Harris, Fort Bend and Montgomery counties, including 1,282 unsheltered. Unsheltered homelessness rose 15.8% from 2024, partly because of reduced shelter capacity, according to the coalition.

I respect that honesty. The coalition connects the setback to shelter capacity and the movement of people into permanent housing. That's a system giving us something to work on, rather than asking us to applaud an old achievement and look away. Keep the gains and the latest setback on the same page. I want to learn from a place that can tell us where it made progress AND where the work needs attention.

## Philadelphia, ask for the follow through

Here at home, the [City of Philadelphia's 2026 Point in Time report](https://www.phila.gov/2026-07-23-philadelphia-2026-point-in-time-count-report-finds-unsheltered-homelessness-drops-for-first-time-in-two-years/) found 711 people unsheltered, down 39% from 1,178. The count took place on February 25, 2026. Fewer people counted outside? That's good news for Philadelphia. I want more of it.

Now put the follow through beside that count. How many people left the street? Were they still housed a year later? What did each of those outcomes cost? Those are the numbers I want in front of me when we talk about the money. Keep the count, too. It tells us something we need to know, and the housing results would help us decide what to do next.

I want whoever presents those results to explain who is included, which spending period applies and how continued housing was checked. Make it possible to see where help lasted and where it broke down. That's a useful demand to make of a system. It gives the people doing the work a clear result to pursue, and keeps the people who need a home at the center of the discussion.

When the next candidate, budget hearing or viral video gives you a cost per homeless person, stop at the division. Ask how many people left the street and were still housed a year later, and what each of those outcomes cost. Get the dates and the method alongside it. Make the speaker answer THAT question.
