Every day, about 115,000 meals and snacks go out from roughly 1,000 public kitchens in Copenhagen: daycares, schools, social services and nursing homes. In 2024, 87.7% of the food those kitchens bought was organic, according to [the city's own 2025 status report on its food and meal strategy](https://maaltider.kk.dk/sites/default/files/2026-01/AfrapporteringMMS_2025_FINAL.pdf). The kitchens that feed children cleared the city's 90% goal. I'm Harry Hayman, I come out of the restaurant business, and the number is not what grabbed me. What grabbed me is how they got there. Copenhagen did not just write a bigger check to its suppliers. It trained its cooks, put less meat on the plate and threw less food away, and it kept doing that for more than twenty years.

## The numbers, from the city that runs the kitchens

There are two primary documents worth reading. The first is the city's report to its own council, the 2025 status report on Copenhagen's food and meal strategy (in Danish). The second is [Copenhagen's submission to the Milan Pact Awards 2025](https://www.milanurbanfoodpolicypact.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/COPENHAGEN_Implementing-Food-Policies_MPA2025.pdf), published by the [Milan Urban Food Policy Pact](https://www.milanurbanfoodpolicypact.org/), a network of cities working on food policy.

They agree on the shape of it. More than 1,000 public kitchens. About 115,000 daily meals, and the city's own report is careful to say that count includes snacks as well as main meals. An organic share of 87.7% in 2024 in the city report, which the Milan submission rounds to a "current average of 87,8%." In the city's table, the Children and Youth Administration bought 91% organic, and its daycares 94%.

The target is written plainly in the strategy: 90% organic, and the gold level of Denmark's [Organic Cuisine Label](https://www.oekologisk-spisemaerke.dk/english) in every kitchen. The first organic target was 75%, set back in 2001. So this is not a pilot that got lucky. It is a long, boring, steady climb, and those are the ones I trust.

One comparison, and I want to label it honestly. In the United States, organic was 6.1% of all food sales in 2025, $70.1 billion, according to the Organic Trade Association's latest market report as covered by [Organic Grower](https://organicgrower.info/news/us-organic-sales-2025-ota-report/) and [NCAT](https://www.ncat.org/news/organic-market-report-shows-strong-growth/). That is every grocery dollar in the country, not school cafeterias, so it is not a like for like number. It just tells you how far from normal Copenhagen's kitchens are.

## The cooks were the plan

Here is the part I would underline twice. The Milan submission calls the targeted upskilling of kitchen staff "a primary tool for turning policy into practice." The city hired culinary advisers, Meyers Madhus, who have run that work since 2016, and it counts the results the way a restaurant counts covers. The Milan submission reports 750 tailored courses for kitchen staff, reaching about 75% of all the city's public kitchens. The city's 2025 report puts the count at 817 courses between 2020 and June 2025, some at the advisers' own kitchen and many held on site.

Then the tools. The city built a free online database of more than 900 recipes that are climate friendly, nutritionally balanced and written for specific ages, so a cook feeding three year olds and a cook feeding residents of a nursing home are not working from the same card. Each kitchen gets an annual local report with its own organic share and climate footprint. The council gets a status report every two years.

Anyone who has run a kitchen knows why this works. You can change the order guide in an afternoon. Changing what forty cooks are comfortable making at seven in the morning takes years of patient, practical help. Copenhagen paid for the help.

It also paid for it in the open. The city's report lists a permanent grant of about DKK 12 million a year for advice and training, plus DKK 17.2 million set aside from 2020 to 2023 for projects. The Milan submission puts the strategy's running budget at about €2 million a year. So organic did not come free. It cost training, and somebody voted for that line in a budget.

## Less meat helped pay the organic premium

Organic food costs more, and the gap is biggest on the most expensive items, meat above all. A study published in January 2025 in Frontiers in Sustainable Food Systems, [Eating green in Copenhagen](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sustainable-food-systems/articles/10.3389/fsufs.2025.1534068/full), looked at how organic buying connects to other habits in Copenhagen households and public kitchens. On the public side, the authors report that meat was reduced to provide "a room for economic manoeuvre" because of the higher organic price premiums. One interviewee put it as creating "a financial leeway for our further organic journey."

The same paper puts Copenhagen's public meals at around 84% organic in 2019. The city kept climbing from there to 87.7% in 2024.

The meat shift kept going for climate reasons too. In 2024 the city's Children and Youth Committee decided that daycares and schools would stop serving red meat from August 2024, according to the city report. The Milan submission reports an average 32% cut in the climate footprint of food purchases, 35% in the children's kitchens, and links it in part to that decision.

I like this mechanism because it is a cook's answer, not an accountant's. Less beef, more beans, lentils and vegetables cooked well, and the money you stop spending on beef buys better carrots. It only works if the cooks know how to make a lentil dish that a six year old will actually eat, which brings us back to the training.

## Waste was the other lever

The second place the money came from was the bin. In one pilot across 20 schools and daycare centres, food waste fell 30%, from about 90 grams to about 60 grams per child per day, according to the Milan submission. Thirty grams sounds like nothing. Multiply it by every child, every school day, every year, and it is real food and real money.

The city has since set itself a target to halve food waste by 2030, and its advisory contract for 2025 to 2028 makes food waste one of the priority areas, the 2025 report says. In other words, the next stretch of this work is aimed at the bin, the same way the last stretch was aimed at the menu.

## The kitchens became classrooms

There is one more piece that matters to me as somebody who believes food is culture, not just fuel. Copenhagen treats the meal as part of the school day. The Milan submission describes children helping prepare and serve meals in schools and daycares, eating together, and purpose built kitchen spaces that let staff, teachers and children work side by side. The city runs "Open Schools" and "Open Day Care" programs with partners including Meyers Madhus and the ØsterGRO rooftop farm, where children learn about growing vegetables, bees and animals.

And the city opens the kitchens to everyone else. Since 2022 it has run an annual food festival inside its public kitchens, where Copenhageners can eat the same meals the children, older residents and vulnerable residents eat every day, and meet the people who cook them. That is how you build a public that will defend the budget line for training.

## What Philadelphia could take from it

Philadelphia already made one big public food decision. Every student in the School District of Philadelphia is offered free school meals with no application, under the federal Community Eligibility Provision, and has been since 2014, as [The Philadelphia Inquirer explains](https://www.inquirer.com/family/free-school-meals-groceries-school-menus-philadelphia-20241004.html). The same Inquirer piece notes that only about half of students take the free lunch. So we have built the access. The question Copenhagen answers is what goes on the tray, and whether kids want to eat it.

I would not start with an organic percentage. I would start where Copenhagen actually started: with the people in the kitchen. Fund real training for school and senior center cooks, measured by courses delivered and kitchens reached, the way Copenhagen counts it. Give every kitchen its own yearly report card, so a cook can see her kitchen's numbers move. Run a waste pilot in twenty schools and publish the grams. And let some of the money saved on meat and waste buy better ingredients from farms in our own region.

None of that needs a law. It needs somebody to treat the people cooking 7 a.m. breakfast as the most important part of the food system, which they are. Copenhagen did. The number followed.
