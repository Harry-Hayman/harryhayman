Four thousand one hundred and two years. That is the number sitting at the bottom of an anniversary email addressed to Harry Hayman, filed in the Innocence Project's records as a monthly donor since long enough ago that I had mostly stopped noticing the charge on my statement. It does not get any easier to read on a fifth pass. Four thousand one hundred and two years is longer than Philadelphia has been an incorporated city, counted twice over, except none of it happened to a city. It happened to two hundred and fifty seven people, one appeal at a time, one late night in a law office at a time, one exhausted family at a time. This month I actually read the numbers instead of scrolling past them, and I want to walk through what they hold, because a few of them run straight through Philadelphia.

## Two Hundred Fifty Seven Times the System Got It Wrong

The Innocence Project keeps a running count on its own site, and this year it reads 257 victories, meaning 257 people who were convicted of crimes they did not commit and are now free, cleared, or both. Two hundred five of those cases turned on DNA evidence, the kind of physical proof that does not care what a jury believed or what a confession said under pressure. The organization's own [exoneration data](https://innocenceproject.org/exonerations-data/) puts the collective time served at 4,102 years, the number I opened with, and it is worth saying plainly that this figure only counts people the Innocence Project itself represented. It is not the whole country's tally. It is one organization's caseload.

That organization started as a legal clinic. [Barry Scheck and Peter Neufeld](https://innocenceproject.org/team/barry-c-scheck-peter-j-neufeld/) founded it in 1992 at the Benjamin N. Cardozo School of Law in New York, working from a fairly simple observation. If DNA testing could prove someone guilty, the same testing could prove someone innocent, and nobody had built an institution whose whole job was to go looking for those cases after the trial had already run its course and the ordinary appeals had already failed. The clinic became an independent nonprofit in 2003 and now sits at the center of the Innocence Network, a group of similar organizations working state by state, described on the project's own [about page](https://innocenceproject.org/about/). Pennsylvania has its own chapter of that network, and I will get to it.

## What Counts as Infrastructure

I think about roads and bridges as infrastructure without having to be told to. A pothole gets reported, a budget line gets argued over, somebody eventually shows up with cones and asphalt. Courts are infrastructure too, and appeals courts and post conviction review boards are the maintenance crew nobody funds properly, because a wrongful conviction does not announce itself the way a collapsed guardrail does. It just sits there, quietly, for years, drawing no attention until somebody with a law degree and a lot of patience goes looking for it.

The [National Registry of Exonerations](https://www.themarshallproject.org/records/2601-national-registry-of-exonerations), a joint project of the University of Michigan, Michigan State and UC Irvine law schools founded in 2012, has logged nearly 3,700 known exonerations across the country since researchers started counting in 1989. In 2024 alone the registry recorded [147 exonerations](https://msutoday.msu.edu/news/2025/04/msu-professors-report-reveals-nearly-150-exonerations-in-2024), an average of thirteen and a half years lost per person, 1,980 years total for that single year by itself. Read those two counts together and the shape of the problem gets clearer. This is not a handful of tragic outliers scattered across four decades. It is a steady, measurable output of a system, the same way traffic counts are the measurable output of a road network, and a steady output means there is a cause somewhere upstream worth finding, whether that turns out to be eyewitness procedure, forensic methods that were weaker than anyone claimed in court, or plain misconduct by the people running the case.

## The Version of This Story in Philadelphia

None of this happens somewhere else, at a comfortable distance. Philadelphia runs its own version of it, on two tracks that rarely get mentioned in the same sentence.

The [Pennsylvania Innocence Project](https://painnocence.org/), housed at [Temple University's Beasley School of Law](https://law.temple.edu/resources/community-service/pa-innocence-project/), does the same work Scheck and Neufeld started, scaled to this state. Students and attorneys there review claims of innocence, litigate the ones that hold up, and in the process train the next round of lawyers to recognize what a wrongful conviction actually looks like from the inside, which turns out to be one of the more effective ways to keep the next one from happening in the first place.

The other track runs through the District Attorney's office itself. Philadelphia's [Conviction Integrity Unit](https://phillyda.org/safety-and-justice/investigations/conviction-integrity-unit-ciu/), formed after the 2018 change in administration, has produced more than 35 exonerations and reversed more than 675 years of imprisonment, with well over a thousand additional convictions still waiting in line for review. This past May, the office [announced its fifty ninth exoneration](https://phillyda.org/news/district-attorney-larry-krasner-announces-exoneration-of-three-co-defendants-for-1997-north-philadelphia-murder/) since that unit began working cases. A 2024 count from [Pennsylvania policy reporting](https://www.cityandstatepa.com/policy/2024/03/philadelphia-no2-exonerations-nationwide/395278/) put Philadelphia second among American cities for total exonerations, 77 of them since researchers began tracking such things in 1990, 47 of those in the years since the Conviction Integrity Unit opened its doors, and the office has kept adding cases since. A prosecutor's office that chooses to look backward at its own convictions is not a small thing to build. Most offices in most cities have never staffed a unit whose entire job is finding out where they got it wrong.

## Why Harry Hayman Gives Monthly Instead of Once

I could write one check a year and feel roughly the same amount of good about it. I do not, and the reason has less to do with any particular generosity on my part than with how casework actually gets funded.

The Innocence Project reports that [58 percent of its funding](https://innocenceproject.org/about/) comes from individual donors, not from a handful of large foundations writing one check that covers everything. That means the organization's caseload, the years it can spend on any single file, the number of investigators it can put on the ground in a state without its own project, tracks reasonably closely to how many people like me keep a recurring donation running quietly in the background. A single gift is a moment. A monthly one is a budget line the organization can actually plan against, closer to how a restaurant plans differently around a regular table than around a walk in off the street.

I have spent a long time arguing, in my food work as much as anywhere, that systems fix broken things and episodic charity does not. This is the same argument pointed at a different building. The [Innocence Advocates program](https://innocenceproject.org/innocence-advocates-donate/), which is the actual name for what I signed up for, exists because somebody at that organization understood that finding a wrongful conviction, building the case, and getting a court to reopen it takes years, not a news cycle, and years need a funding shape that matches them. Next month the charge goes through again, quietly, the way it has for a while now, and somewhere in Pennsylvania a case file that has already waited eight or nine years for a hearing moves one folder closer to the top of a very long stack.
