At the corner of Front and Ritner in the Whitman section of South Philadelphia, a room that had spent years as a deteriorating corner bar opened its doors in early May 2026 as something the neighborhood had not quite seen before. The walls are dark, the wood bar is wide and commanding, and stained glass catches the light the way old rooms do when they have been allowed to grow into themselves. Harry Hayman walked in on a recent night not knowing exactly what to expect, and walked out talking about the people as much as the food. That, in his reading, is the real sign.

[The Philadelphia Inquirer's August 2026 review](https://www.inquirer.com/food/waltz-italian-south-philadelphia-dougherty-whitman-20260813.html) described Waltz as a restaurant that already feels like an old haunt despite having been open only a few months. It fills with regulars, with tables of longtime friends, with young couples bringing their parents, with rooms that stay occupied well past the time anyone planned to leave. Harry Hayman found all of that on the night he was there, along with some of the more interesting cooking South Philadelphia has seen in recent years.

## The House on Front and Ritner

The building at 2351 S. Front Street had been Anthony's Crab House before Joe Dougherty and his family acquired it in September 2024. It was a corner bar, one the neighborhood had watched decline over years, and the new owners initially considered keeping it as a dive bar before deciding to go further. What they found when they opened the walls was a building worth saving. Workers discovered 1924 editions of the Philadelphia Inquirer used as insulation, the kind of discovery that shifts a renovation from a business decision into something closer to an obligation. The Doughertys restored the exterior brickwork, rebuilt the interior, and gave the room a look that lands somewhere between the Italian supper clubs of the neighborhood's past and something the block had never had before.

[The Inquirer's first report on Waltz in June 2026](https://www.inquirer.com/food/restaurants/waltz-italian-restaurant-south-philadelphia-20260601.html) found a wood-fired oven visible from the dining room, stained-glass windows, and fresh mozzarella and burrata made daily and discarded at the end of service rather than held over. That discipline in the kitchen, the refusal to reuse what was made the day before, is what Harry Hayman describes as the kind of detail you notice only when you know what to look for.

He read the stuffed crabs on the menu as a nod to the building's past as Anthony's Crab House. That is his reading, not a stated intention of the kitchen, but it is the kind of reading a room earns when it has been built with enough attention to its own history.

## The People at the Center of It

Joe Dougherty is 25 years old. His family runs Dougherty Electric, a contracting business operating near the restaurant, which meant that when he walked into the building on Front and Ritner he was not entering from outside the neighborhood. He was a South Philly businessman who knew how to look at a structure and understand what it could become.

His fiancee Celine Tobin brought direct hospitality experience to the project. She is a Temple University graduate in tourism and hospitality management who had worked in food and beverage at the Union League Golf Club in Torresdale before the restaurant took shape. The name of the place grew out of a conversation between them. Dougherty had settled on "Walt's" as a reference to the Whitman neighborhood and the Walt Whitman Bridge. Tobin, as the renovation grew more refined and the room began to look like a restaurant rather than a bar, suggested swapping the S for a Z. As the Inquirer reported, the thinking was that as the renovation got nicer and classier, Waltz made sense as a reference to ballroom dancing. It is the kind of naming decision that only reads as obvious after the fact, once you are already inside the room it describes.

In the kitchen, chef Sergio Mateo leads a brigade whose experience comes from Philadelphia fine dining. His resume includes time at Ambra and Whetstone, two restaurants that ask serious things of their kitchens. That training is visible in the food. The branzino comes from an 800-degree wood-fired oven. The tiramisu is assembled to order rather than set out hours before service. The mozzarella is made fresh before each dinner and not carried over to the next day.

## What Harry Hayman Found on the Plate

The stuffed crabs were what Harry Hayman described as one of the absolute stars of the evening. They are already becoming one of Waltz's signature dishes, which is the kind of reputation a kitchen earns by getting a dish right, not by calling attention to it. The crab preparation exists within a long tradition in South Philadelphia, where the proximity of the Chesapeake Bay watershed and the Italian-American kitchen produced dishes that combined shellfish with the flavors the neighborhood already knew. Chesapeake Bay blue crab began arriving in Philadelphia by refrigerated rail in the 1870s, and it has appeared in Italian-American cooking in the city in one form or another ever since.

The whole branzino came from the wood-fired oven with tomato, capers, and heat. Branzino, the European sea bass known scientifically as Dicentrarchus labrax, has been a centerpiece of Mediterranean cooking for centuries, prized for its white, delicate flesh and its capacity to carry assertive flavors. At 800 degrees, the wood-fired hearth at Waltz produces a result that a conventional kitchen cannot. The capers and tomato are not a garnish here; they are the point.

The tiramisu came with the ladyfingers dunked in coffee to order. Tiramisu traces its origins to the Veneto region of northeastern Italy, where the name in Venetian dialect means to lift me up. The dessert has been made everywhere from home kitchens to formal dining rooms since the late 1960s, and its defining quality is the balance between the bitterness of the coffee, the richness of the mascarpone, and the texture of the soaked ladyfinger. Making it to order rather than hours before service is a labor decision that costs more than it saves. Harry Hayman's instruction was simply this: do not skip it.

The bar program ran to Fluffy Negroni Sours, Blackberry-Sage Margaritas, and Pepperoncini Tinis. [The Infatuation's review of Waltz](https://www.theinfatuation.com/philadelphia/reviews/waltz-philly), which awarded the restaurant an 8.1 rating, called the Pepperoncini Martini a standout and noted that the cocktail program showed Waltz knows how to have fun behind the bar. Harry Hayman had no argument with that assessment.

## The Story Behind the Name

The Whitman neighborhood takes its name from the [Walt Whitman Bridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walt_Whitman_Bridge), the steel suspension bridge designed by civil engineer Othmar Ammann that opened to traffic on May 16, 1957, connecting Philadelphia to Gloucester City in Camden County, New Jersey. The bridge was named for the American poet and essayist Walt Whitman, who spent the final years of his life in nearby Camden. Before the bridge gave the neighborhood its current name, the area around Front Street's southernmost reach was one of the city's quieter pockets, close to the port and bordered by rail yards, not a neighborhood that drew attention from across the city.

That geography carries into the present. The [Whitman neighborhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitman,_Philadelphia) sits at Philadelphia's southeastern edge, bounded on the east by Front Street, on the west by Sixth Street, and on the south by Bigler Street. It is near the port, near the railyards, near the bridge. Joe Dougherty grew up knowing that geography. When he chose "Walt's" as a working name, he was reaching for something local, something that would mean something to people who already lived in the shadow of the bridge. What Celine Tobin saw when she suggested the Z was that the room they were building had outgrown an inside reference. It deserved a word with its own life, one that carried music and movement and a particular kind of confidence.

[Visit Philadelphia recognized Waltz on its list of the city's best new restaurants for Summer 2026](https://www.visitphilly.com/articles/philadelphia/new-restaurants/), a recognition that arrived within months of the restaurant opening its doors.

## South Philadelphia and Its Italian Table

The Italian-American cooking tradition in South Philadelphia is among the oldest in the country. Italian immigrants, mostly from southern regions including Calabria, Sicily, and Campania, began settling in the blocks around Washington Avenue and Ninth Street from the late 1880s onward, bringing cooking traditions that adapted over generations to the ingredients of a new city. The [Encyclopedia of Greater Philadelphia documents South Philadelphia's Italian-American community](https://philadelphiaencyclopedia.org/locations/south-philadelphia/) as one of the oldest and most intact urban immigrant enclaves in the United States.

[Ralph's Italian Restaurant](https://ralphsrestaurant.com/history/), which Francesco Dispigno opened on South Ninth Street in 1900, holds the distinction of being the oldest continuously operating Italian restaurant in the country. A fourth and fifth generation of the same family still runs it today. What emerged from that century of cooking in the neighborhood was a version of Italian-American food calibrated to a specific place: red gravy made with patience, pasta cut by hand, shellfish from the nearby bay folded into preparations that had no precise Italian precedent but tasted like they belonged. Waltz sits within that tradition while drawing on the fine dining background of its kitchen, which means it is neither a nostalgia project nor a rejection of what came before it.

## What It Takes to Open Right Now

The [Philadelphia Inquirer reported in January 2026](https://www.inquirer.com/food/restaurants/philadelphia-restaurant-trends-2026-20260107.html) that the city was looking at more than a hundred new restaurant openings during the year, following an equally active 2025. [Philadelphia Magazine observed in July 2026](https://www.phillymag.com/restaurants/2026/07/28/philadelphia-restaurant-boom/) that the city was experiencing something close to a restaurant gold rush, with new rooms opening in neighborhoods that had not previously drawn much dining attention from across the city. The Whitman section of South Philadelphia, at Front and Ritner, is precisely that kind of neighborhood.

Opening a restaurant in that environment requires something beyond confidence. Food and labor costs across the American restaurant industry rose 35 percent over the five years leading into 2026, according to the National Restaurant Association. Occupancy costs were up 14 percent from 2019. A 25-year-old from a family electrical contracting business, working alongside a hospitality-trained partner and a kitchen led by a chef with serious fine dining experience, is attempting something that professionals with decades of background approach with caution.

What Harry Hayman observed at Waltz on the night he was there was not the careful execution of a concept. It was a room that had figured out how to make the people in it feel like they belonged there. The stuffed crabs, the branzino, the tiramisu made to order: those are the reasons a table fills. The hospitality is the reason it stays. On Front and Ritner, at the southernmost edge of the city, the dance has barely started.
