Philadelphia, I have a question I want answered on Monday night, and I am not the one who gets to ask it. I'm Harry Hayman, and I have been thinking about what Terry Gross will say to Paul McCartney that makes him stop for a second and admit he has never thought about it that way. That is the moment I am hoping for. McCartney has spent more than six decades telling stories about the Beatles, songwriting and a life most of us can barely picture. Gross has spent 50 years listening closely enough to know when a better story sits one question away. I have not been to this event yet. I am writing before the night, with a seat on my calendar and a lot of anticipation, because this one is happening in our own city and it is worth a few hundred words.

## What Is Happening on Monday

On Monday, November 23, 2026, WHYY is hosting a Philadelphia watch party at WHYY Studios, 150 N. 6th Street. According to the [WHYY registration page](https://support.whyy.org/page/103541/event/1), doors open at 6:30 p.m., Cherri Gregg hosts the room, and the evening includes a cocktail reception, dinner and dessert, along with a live simulcast of Terry Gross's conversation with Paul McCartney.

The conversation itself is happening in New York. [MyChesCo's report on the announcement](https://www.mychesco.com/a/news/regional/whyy-taps-paul-mccartney-for-new-york-fundraising-event/) says WHYY is presenting a live conversation between McCartney and Gross at The Town Hall in Manhattan, at 123 West 43rd Street, about his life and music, including his work with the Beatles, Wings and as a solo artist, with all proceeds benefiting public media. The same report notes that the evening marks Gross's 50th anniversary with Fresh Air, and that McCartney arrives with a new album, The Boys of Dungeon Lane, his first new solo album in more than five years.

So the Philadelphia room is not a consolation prize. It is the hometown version of the same night, hosted by the station where the show was born.

## A Philadelphia Show Before It Was an American One

Here is the part I think a lot of people outside the building do not know. Fresh Air did not start as a national institution. According to [WHYY's history of the show](https://whyy.org/articles/fresh-air-50th-anniversary/), it began in 1973 when station manager David Karpoff launched it, with Judy Blank hosting at first and volunteer staff able to sign up to do interviews. The station was then called WUHY, mostly classical music with a small audience.

The leap came in 1985, when NPR was looking for a strong opening act for All Things Considered and station manager Bill Siemering pitched the local program nationally. NPR first carried it as a weekly half hour. Then, per the same WHYY history, in 1987 the Corporation for Public Broadcasting granted WHYY $5 million over five years to build it into an hour long daily show. The [Philadelphia Inquirer's 2017 look at the show's archive](https://inquirer.com/archive/john_timpane/fresh-air-fresh-forever-30-years-as-national-show-new-digital-archive-20170606.html) puts the start of the daily national program at May 11, 1987.

Gross herself has said the same thing out loud. In [WHYY's account of her visit to The Late Show with Stephen Colbert](https://whyy.org/articles/terry-gross-fresh-air-stephen-colbert/), she credits the Corporation for Public Broadcasting with helping turn Fresh Air from a local program into a nationally recognized public radio show. A show that Philadelphia built, and that public funding helped carry out of the city, is now hosting a fundraiser to support public media. I find that circle pretty satisfying.

## How the Interviews Actually Get Made

I love what the WHYY history reveals about the process, because we only ever hear the polished result. According to WHYY, staff select guests and compile research for Gross and the show's other interviewers. Interviews can run 90 minutes or more, and editors cut them down to about 40 minutes for broadcast. So the calm, unhurried quality you hear on the radio is the product of a long conversation and careful editing, not a short chat.

WHYY also reports that Dave Davies, who interviews for the show, learned from Gross to plan an interview in chapters, such as early life, breakthrough and current project. She also told him that nothing she tells him should interfere with him being himself. I think that is the most useful interviewing advice I have seen in a while, and it applies well beyond radio. Know the map, then listen to the person in front of you.

The Inquirer adds a detail I did not expect. Gross was only 24 when she started hosting, and she says she felt she had to reassure guests that she was a professional. Fifty years later, guests presumably need no reassurance.

## The Scale of Fifty Years

The numbers around the show are worth sitting with. WHYY says the program has run more than 15,000 interviews, reaches 3.7 million people weekly on 651 radio stations, and is downloaded 1.6 million times a week as a podcast. It also reports that guests have included four U.S. presidents and more than 300 Pulitzer Prize winners, and that in 2016 President Barack Obama awarded Gross the National Humanities Medal.

None of those figures tells you what it feels like to hear a good question. But they do tell you how many people have spent a part of their day in her company. Add the hometown pride of it being a Philadelphia program, produced by WHYY's FM station, and you start to understand why this evening feels bigger than a concert link.

## Why a Room Like This Is Worth Leaving the House For

I spend a lot of time thinking about what makes a room worth leaving the house for, and this is a damn good example. You could stream a conversation with a Beatle at home. But you would miss the people around you. At the registration page, a ticket is listed at $125 with an option to add a donation, and each guest receives a commemorative gift. What you are really buying is a seat in a room full of people who care enough about a radio show to put on a jacket and come downtown on a Monday.

And afterward, the good part. You turn to the person next to you and ask, did you hear what he just said? That sentence is the whole reason watch parties exist. A conversation you have been waiting for becomes a conversation you get to have.

## What I Am Listening For

Nobody knows yet what Gross will ask or what McCartney will answer. That is the point of a live conversation. What I am listening for is the follow up, the second question that shows she heard the first answer. Fifty years of interviews, by WHYY's count, and the craft still comes down to that.

Congratulations, Terry. Fifty years in, and I still want to hear your next question. If you are in Philadelphia on Monday, [register for the watch party here](https://support.whyy.org/page/103541/event/1) and come find the room.
