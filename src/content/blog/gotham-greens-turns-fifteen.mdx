I keep a mental file of businesses that quietly kept working while everyone else was writing about the ones that didn't. Gotham Greens is the newest entry in it. Fifteen years ago the company's entire footprint was one greenhouse bolted to a rooftop in Brooklyn. This year, [Gotham Greens marked its fifteenth year in business](https://www.gothamgreens.com/recent-press/) with a very different set of numbers: more than forty acres of greenhouse, spread across nine states, feeding more than 6,500 stores. I do not run Gotham Greens and I have no relationship with them through Concrete Greens, the food infrastructure venture I help build here in Philadelphia. I noticed the story because it is the exact shape of the argument I keep making about controlled environment agriculture, except this time somebody else's balance sheet is doing the arguing for me.

## One Rooftop, One Bet

Gotham Greens started the way a lot of good Brooklyn ideas start: small, specific, and load bearing on one roof. The company's own account of [its founding story](https://www.gothamgreens.com/our-story/) places the flagship greenhouse in Greenpoint in 2011, growing lettuce and herbs above a neighborhood most people only knew from the outside. It was not a research lab. It was a working greenhouse next to real customers, and that turns out to be the whole story.

I think about that distinction more than I probably should. A research lab proves a technology works. A working greenhouse next to real customers proves a business works, and those are not the same test. Fifteen years later, Gotham Greens is running production across New York, Illinois, Rhode Island, Maryland, Virginia, Colorado, California, Georgia, and Texas, nine states in total, at more than forty acres and roughly 1.8 million square feet of growing space under climate control, according to [Perishable News](https://perishablenews.com/produce/gotham-greens-marks-15-years-with-record-growth-national-expansion-and-continued-category-leadership/). Every one of those states got added because a market showed up first, not because a spreadsheet said it should exist.

## What the Obituaries Actually Say

I have read a lot of controlled environment agriculture failure stories this year, and on this account I try never to name a specific company when I am making a general point, because that reads like I am settling a score I have no part in. What I can say is that the pattern in the trade coverage is consistent, and it has almost nothing to do with whether the technology worked.

[iGrow News](https://igrownews.com/controlled-environment-agriculture-business-model/) published an analysis this year making a case I have not seen argued as directly anywhere else: the businesses that failed were not undercapitalized, and several had raised tens of millions of dollars behind technology that worked as promised. The problem sat one level up, in the structure of the business itself. Running a controlled environment agriculture company means running a technology company, a real estate company, and a consumer goods company at the same time, and most of the failures were only built to run one of those three well. A separate analysis from [AgTechNavigator](https://www.agnavigator.com/Article/2026/01/28/why-agtech-start-ups-failed-last-year-and-a-playbook-for-2026/), looking at agtech shutdowns more broadly, found that capital could extend a company's runway but could not fix its underlying economics, and that half the shutdowns it tracked traced back directly to cost and adoption problems rather than a broken product.

Put plainly: raising money to build growing capacity before the customer is lined up to buy what comes out of it is a bet on a forecast. Building capacity because the orders already exist is a bet on a customer already standing in front of you. Those are two different businesses wearing the same industry label, and only one of them tends to still be operating in year fifteen.

## The Company That Never Made the List

Here is where Gotham Greens' math gets specific enough to matter. More than forty acres, about 1.8 million square feet, nine states, and more than 6,500 retail doors nationwide, a list that runs from Whole Foods and Kroger to Sprouts, Albertsons, FreshDirect, Harris Teeter, Publix, Amazon Fresh, and Target. The company reports its market share growing 15 percent year over year, and it just handed the CEO seat to Craig Stevenson while cofounder Viraj Puri moves into an executive chairman role, according to the company's own [fifteenth anniversary announcement](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/04/16/3275510/0/en/gotham-greens-celebrates-15th-anniversary-and-announces-new-leadership-viraj-puri-to-become-executive-chairman-craig-stevenson-named-ceo.html), a leadership move also covered independently by [Blue Book Services](https://www.bluebookservices.com/gotham-greens-names-new-ceo-and-executive-chairman/). That is the kind of transition a company plans when it expects to keep operating, not when it is managing a wind down.

None of that happened in one leap. It happened one state, one retailer relationship, and one growing season at a time, over fifteen years, starting from a single rooftop. I find that unglamorous in a way I actually respect. Nobody writes a feature story about a company that grew exactly as fast as its own orders did. Slow, funded growth does not come with a dramatic headline attached to it. It just comes with a balance sheet that still works fifteen years later, which is a better prize than the headline ever was.

## Selling Into Demand That Already Exists

I think about this the same way I think about a jazz club building its season. A club that books six months out on a hunch about who might sell tickets is making the grow first, earn later bet. A club that expands its calendar because a Tuesday night series has quietly sold out four times running is making the other bet, the one Gotham Greens made nine times over, state by state. Neither approach is guaranteed to work. But one of them fails safely, at the size of a single slow Tuesday, and the other fails at the size of a fully built greenhouse nobody ordered anything from.

I do not think controlled environment agriculture is mainly a technology story anymore, and I am not sure that is a popular thing to say inside this sector. The technology has worked for years now. What decides whether a company is still standing in year fifteen is whether it grew at the speed its own customers were actually asking for, and whether it built more than one way to make money along the way.

## What Concrete Greens Takes From This

I want to be direct about why I am spending this many words on a company I have no stake in. Concrete Greens is not a start up chasing a forecast, and it is not a charity either. It is a break even, revenue generating piece of food infrastructure that complements the farms already feeding this region rather than trying to replace them, growing food locally and year round, and putting whatever surplus it produces back into the Philadelphia neighborhoods that need it most. That is a smaller version of the exact discipline Gotham Greens has been running for fifteen years: sell into demand you can already see, grow at the pace of that demand, and let the balance sheet do the talking instead of the pitch deck.

I do not know yet whether this city fully believes that model can scale the way the coastal greenhouse companies have. I am honestly still working that question out myself, in public, as I write this. But I know what fifteen years of unglamorous, demand matched growth looks like when I read about it happening somewhere else, and I would rather build toward that than toward a headline. A city that wants local, year round food does not need another company betting on a forecast. It needs more companies willing to grow only as fast as the orders actually come in, the same slow math Gotham Greens has been running since one rooftop in Brooklyn.
