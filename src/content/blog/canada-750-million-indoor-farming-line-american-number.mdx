There is a photograph I keep going back to. A long glass greenhouse near Leamington, Ontario, shot on a December night, the whole thing glowing gold from the inside while the field and the sky around it are black. The person who took it wrote underneath that [Essex County is home to huge greenhouses growing all kinds of veggies and plants year round](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Greenhouse_economy_%284179696789%29.jpg). On 11 June 2026, Canada's Prime Minister turned that glow into national policy. The [National Food Security Strategy he launched](https://www.pm.gc.ca/en/news/news-releases/2026/06/11/prime-minister-carney-launches-national-food-security-strategy) gave year round growing in greenhouses, vertical farms and other enclosed spaces its own line: C$750 million. I am Harry Hayman, I write about food systems in Philadelphia, and my first question when I read it was the obvious one. If somebody asked an American indoor farmer for the federal number, what would they say? I went looking. The short answer is that there is no single number to give, and that tells you something.

## What Canada Actually Committed

Every Canadian figure in this piece is in Canadian dollars, and I have not converted any of them.

The strategy itself is the country's first. Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada describes it as [$3.2 billion over 10 years](https://agriculture.canada.ca/en/department/initiatives/national-food-security-strategy) across four pillars: grocery competition, processing food closer to where it is grown, growing fruit and vegetables year round, and cutting regulatory delays. The Prime Minister's release rounds it to "more than $3 billion". Inside it sits the line I care about. The release says the government will "invest $750 million to drastically expand year-round Canadian production of fruits and vegetables", and it names greenhouses, vertical farms and other enclosed growing spaces as the way to do it. It also delivers immediate expensing for new or expanded greenhouse construction, so a grower can write off the cost of the building up front.

The detail came on 30 October. Minister Heath MacDonald [launched the Controlled Environment Agriculture Growth Pathway Program](https://www.canada.ca/en/agriculture-agri-food/news/2026/10/government-of-canada-invests-750-million-in-domestic-year-round-food-production-to-improve-access-to-fruits-and-vegetables.html), and the [program page](https://agriculture.canada.ca/en/programs/controlled-environment-agriculture-growth-pathway) lays it out: C$750 million over seven years, running to 31 March 2033, in two streams.

The big one is C$650 million for technology adoption: automation, robotics, lighting and digital growing tools, for producers building or modernising facilities. The [stream page](https://agriculture.canada.ca/en/programs/controlled-environment-agriculture-growth-pathway/technology-adoption-stream) says a project can receive from C$100,000 up to C$10 million, with the government normally covering at most half the eligible costs, and up to 70 percent for projects that grow new crops to replace imports and are led by underrepresented groups. The smaller one is C$100 million for rural and northern communities, as non repayable contributions for community driven growing projects.

Applications are open right now. Project summary forms are due by noon Eastern on 23 December 2026.

## The Targets Canada Wrote Down

What I respect most is that the strategy put numbers on the outcome, not just the spending. The [key performance indicators on the strategy page](https://agriculture.canada.ca/en/department/initiatives/national-food-security-strategy) are specific:

Double the value of controlled environment production sold in Canada, from C$774 million in 2024 to C$1.55 billion in 2032.

Cut dependence on imported crops that could be grown this way by 20 percent by 2032.

Lower labour and energy costs in indoor production by 10 to 20 percent by 2032.

These are targets. Nobody has hit them yet. But they are written where anybody can check them in 2032, which is more than most food policy promises.

The same page also explains why Canada bothered. In 2024 the country had 974 commercial greenhouse vegetable operations with C$2.7 billion in sales, and about 71 percent of those sales by value went to the United States. Read that twice. A lot of the greenhouse cucumbers, tomatoes and peppers in American grocery stores were grown under glass in Ontario and British Columbia. Canada is not starting from zero. It is putting public money behind something it already does well and asking it to feed Canadians first.

## Committed Is Not Spent

I want to be careful here, because a headline number is easy to cheer.

The Arrell Food Institute at the University of Guelph [called the strategy](https://arrellfoodinstitute.ca/assessing-canadas-national-food-security-strategy/) "a step toward treating food as essential national infrastructure." In the same piece it said, "The next test will be implementation." That is exactly right. The intake opened on 30 October and closes on 23 December, so as of today the C$750 million is committed and open for applications. It has not been handed out.

One correction to something I had read. In September the minister announced [up to C$894,673 for SmartGRO](https://www.canada.ca/en/agriculture-agri-food/news/2026/09/minister-macdonald-announces-up-to-nearly-895000-to-develop-and-test-new-clean-technology-to-benefit-canadas-indoor-agriculture-sector.html), a Calgary start up testing pulsing LED grow lights at a greenhouse there. It is a real indoor farming grant, but it came from the Agricultural Clean Technology Program, not from the C$750 million. The money from the new line has not started to move yet. When it does, the first list of funded projects will tell us far more than the launch did.

## The American Number

So, what is the American number? There is no single federal envelope for controlled environment agriculture. What we have are pieces, and they are small.

The Congressional Research Service compared current law with the [House passed and Senate committee bills in its report R48918](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-11-10_R48918_dbf6dbf844f56c290c9ca1d3c7c680c04a818ddb.html), updated 10 November. Under current law, the urban, indoor and innovative production research grants got US$2 million in mandatory money for fiscal 2026, plus authority for up to US$10 million a year more. USDA's Office of Urban Agriculture and Innovative Production is authorized at US$10 million a year. Authorized is not appropriated. Congress can authorize a program and then fund it with less, or nothing.

None of that is a CEA line. Those programs cover urban farms, rooftop gardens and community plots as much as greenhouses. Indoor growers also reach federal money through crop insurance, USDA research grants and specialty crop programs, so I will not tell you the United States spends some tiny amount on indoor farming. It spends money on indoor farming through a dozen doors, and nobody has added it up into one number with a target attached.

Both farm bills would move things a little. CRS says both would add hydroponics, aquaponics, aeroponics and other controlled environment production technologies to the research priorities of the Agriculture and Food Research Initiative, and both would expand the urban agriculture office's mission, with the House text naming controlled environment agriculture systems in it. The Senate committee bill would authorize US$18 million a year through 2031 for the urban, indoor and emerging production research initiative.

Then there is the calendar. The House passed its bill 224 to 200 on 30 April. The Senate Agriculture Committee voted 12 to 11 on 16 September to report its version. The last extension of the 2018 farm bill ran out on 30 September, and [DTN reported](https://www.dtnpf.com/agriculture/web/ag/news/article/2026/10/01/crp-enrollment-paused-farm-bill) that the stopgap funding bill "does not extend the farm bill." As of this week, [Pro Farmer reports](https://www.profarmer.com/pro-farmer-max/evening-report/evening-report-trump-urges-republicans-complete-farm-bill) the Senate version still has not reached the floor.

So Canada has a seven year line with a target. We have an expired law, two bills that disagree, and some research priorities with CEA written into them. That is the honest comparison.

## Pennsylvania's Line, If It Had One

Pennsylvania is not empty handed. The state's Urban Agriculture Infrastructure Grants have put [more than US$3.7 million into 180 projects since 2019](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/pda/newsroom/2026-urban-ag-tour--investing-to-expand-opportunity--alleviate-u), buying greenhouses, rooftop gardens and irrigation. The same release says the state's US$10 million Agricultural Innovation Grants went to 88 businesses, including a high tech aquaponic and hydroponic greenhouse in Erie's poorest zip code. And this state already grows a huge crop indoors: mushrooms around Kennett Square, which I wrote about in [the circular mushroom farm piece](https://harryhayman.com/blog/circular-mushroom-farm-pennsylvania/).

But those are grants, not a line. None of them says: here is what we want indoor growing to produce in Pennsylvania by 2032, and here is the money behind it. I have also seen the other side of this. [80 Acres Farms reached 18,000 stores and still could not raise the money](https://harryhayman.com/blog/80-acres-farms-capital-failure-vertical-farming/). When indoor farming fails, it is usually not the technology. It is the business model is built for venture capital instead of for feeding people, and that is a financing problem a public line can actually address.

That is the model I keep pushing at Concrete Greens: indoor growing as infrastructure that pays its own way, year round food grown close to the people eating it, alongside the farms we already have, with the surplus going to neighbours who need it. A state line with targets would make that easier to build here.

So here is my question for Harrisburg, and for anyone in Philadelphia who cares where our winter greens come from. Canada wrote down C$750 million, seven years, and three targets. What would Pennsylvania's line for indoor growing be, and what would we promise it would produce?
