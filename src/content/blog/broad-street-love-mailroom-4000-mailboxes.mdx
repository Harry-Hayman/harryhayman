More than 4,000 people get their mail at one address on South Broad Street. I keep turning that number over. It is not an apartment tower or a post office. It is a stone church between Spruce and Pine, and the people who use that address are, for the most part, people who do not have one of their own. For a lot of them, it is also how their food arrives. I am Harry Hayman, and I write I Am Hungry in Philadelphia to look at hunger as infrastructure rather than charity. This building is one of the clearest pieces of that infrastructure I know of, and almost nobody counts it as such.

## The address that holds a city's worth of mail

The building at 315 South Broad Street went up as the Chambers Wylie Memorial Presbyterian Church, an English Gothic design by Rankin and Kellogg completed in 1901, according to the [Wikimedia Commons record of the building](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Broad_Street_Ministry.jpg). Since 2005 it has been home to the organization most of us knew as Broad Street Ministry. On May 14, 2024 it took a new name, Broad Street Love, as its [2024 Impact Report](https://broadstreetlove.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/2024-Impact-Report-Final.pdf) explains, with the mission and services unchanged.

In October, Broad Street Love wrote that its [mailroom is home to over 4,000 active mailboxes](https://broadstreetlove.org/about-us/news/civic-engagement-at-bsl-an-avenue-for-empowerment/), and that this makes 315 South Broad Street the address with the most registered voters of any single address in Pennsylvania. That is their count, and it is a staggering one. The same Impact Report says the mailroom opened 4,211 new mailboxes in 2024 alone.

Think about what that means in practice. A neighborhood post office handles mail for blocks of houses that each have a door and a slot. Here the doors and slots are rows of boxes in a church, sorted by hand. The organization's [mail volunteer page](https://broadstreetlove.org/volunteer/mail-service/) asks for people who can read fluently and bend over to sort, and adds that alphabetization skills and friendly faces matter too. That is the whole machine.

## Why food benefits run on paper and stamps

Here is the part that most people never have to think about. The systems that feed people who are struggling still talk to them by mail. Renewal forms, requests for documents, approval letters and closure notices land in an envelope at an address on file.

Pennsylvania showed us what happens when that envelope is late. The state Department of Human Services says that [some DHS mail was delayed from November 3 to December 3, 2025](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/dhs/contact/mail-delay) because of a failure at an outside mail vendor. For Medicaid, the state could reopen cases and push deadlines. For SNAP, the federal food benefit many of us still call food stamps, the department wrote that "DHS is not permitted to extend renewal deadlines for SNAP recipients" under federal law and USDA guidance. People whose cases closed had to send documents, appeal or apply again.

So the mail is not a side detail. If a letter does not reach you, a deadline can pass without you ever seeing it. And if you sleep in a shelter, in a car or on a friend's couch this month and somewhere else next month, the question of where that letter goes is the whole game.

Broad Street Love's [mail program page](https://broadstreetlove.org/our-services/mail/) puts it plainly: a mailing address helps guests establish residency, get identification, search for work and receive benefits, and the people who use the service "are more likely to obtain benefits and health insurance." In 2023, according to the same page, the mailroom passed on 1,692 identity documents and birth certificates and 1,565 benefits documents, covering Medicare and Medicaid, SSI, SSDI and more.

One guest quoted in the 2024 Impact Report said it in a sentence I cannot shake: "I'm going to use this address tomorrow to get emergency food stamps." That is the hunger system working, and it is working because a nonprofit in a former church keeps a box with that person's name on it.

## A full lunch, served at the table

The mail is half of the model. The other half is lunch, and it is served in a way I wish more of the city copied. The [meals page](https://broadstreetlove.org/our-services/meals/) describes lunch for over 300 guests every weekday, cooked by professionals with ingredients from the Carversville Farm Foundation, eaten as a full sit down meal served restaurant style. The Impact Report describes guests gathered at round tables with volunteer wait staff in the old sanctuary, and counts 86,580 meals served there in 2024.

I was at their Open House last year and [wrote about the people who make it run](https://harryhayman.com/blog/broad-street-love-where-compassion-isn-t-just-talk-it-s-action). What I keep coming back to is the service itself. You do not carry a tray to a counter. You sit, and a volunteer brings lunch to your table.

The need has been climbing. In an [October 2025 SNAP update](https://broadstreetlove.org/about-us/news/snap-update/), during the federal shutdown, Broad Street Love reported daily meal attendance reaching 300 to 400 people, with up to 200 in the first hour alone, and noted that for about 20 percent of its guests, that lunch is their only meal of the day. In July, the new chief executive wrote that [meal attendance had hit more than 350 people per day](https://broadstreetlove.org/about-us/news/with-appreciation-from-ceo-otis-hackney/) in recent weeks.

The two halves feed each other. The organization says its meal is the gateway to everything else: you come for lunch, you start to trust the place, and then you open a mailbox. The same guest who talked about food stamps put it in order: "I came here for the food and now I use the mail, too."

## A new chief executive who built city systems

Since May 12, 2026, Broad Street Love has been led by Otis Hackney. The board's [announcement of his appointment](https://broadstreetlove.org/about-us/news/announcing-otis-hackney-as-next-ceo-of-broad-street-love/) describes a lifelong Philadelphian who began as a math teacher and became a high school principal, notably at South Philadelphia High School, and most recently led the national I Have a Dream Foundation.

In between, he ran education policy for the City. Philadelphia [appointed him Chief Education Officer in December 2015](https://www.phila.gov/2022-10-19-mayor-kenney-announces-departure-of-chief-education-officer-otis-hackney/), and in that role he oversaw the launch of PHLpreK, the City funded pre kindergarten program, and the creation of Community Schools with the School District, before leaving at the end of October 2022.

I find that background hopeful for a specific reason. Pre K and community schools are both cases where the city decided that something families used to piece together on their own was public infrastructure. Somebody who has made that argument inside City Hall knows what it takes to move a service from favor to system.

## Who should be paying for the address

Here is where I am thinking out loud, and this is my opinion, not a sourced fact.

We count food pantries, meals and SNAP dollars when we talk about hunger in Philadelphia. We almost never count the address. Yet by Broad Street Love's own numbers, one South Broad Street building does a job for more than 4,000 people that, for most of us, the post office and our landlord do without a second thought. Broad Street Love says its work is [made possible by donors, partners and volunteers](https://broadstreetlove.org/about-us/), and on its [volunteer story page](https://broadstreetlove.org/about-us/news/love-in-action-how-volunteers-help-bring-radical-hospitality-to-life/) it counts 25 to 40 volunteers joining staff every day.

So my question is simple. If an address is part of how Philadelphians get fed, who should be paying for the mailroom? I do not know the right answer. Maybe it is a line in the City's homeless services budget. Maybe it is a state contract, given that the state's own benefit letters are what fill those boxes. Maybe it is a partnership with the Postal Service. What I am fairly sure of is that a system which depends on paper reaching people should not treat the place where that paper lands as a nice extra.

The good news is that the model already exists, and it is working on South Broad Street. Philadelphia does not need to invent it. We need to decide whether it is part of the plumbing.
