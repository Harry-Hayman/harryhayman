In January, about 7,000 new people came to one Philadelphia food bank for help. In July, about 30,000 new people came to the same food bank, in a single month.

Those two numbers are the clearest picture I have found of what the SNAP cuts look like on the ground in this city. I want to lay out where they come from, what has happened since, and what I take from them, because a number this big is easy to read past.

## Who Is Counting

The food bank is [Share Food Program](https://www.sharefoodprogram.org/). It started in 1986 as a food cooperative for Philadelphians who could not get affordable groceries, and it now works out of a warehouse on West Hunting Park Avenue. By [its own account](https://www.sharefoodprogram.org/about-us/) it distributed more than 30 million meals in 2024 and feeds hundreds of thousands of neighbors each month, more than half of them children and seniors. It serves five counties across greater Philadelphia, mostly by supplying the neighborhood pantries people actually walk into.

The January and July figures come from Jess Bautista, Share's chief communications officer, speaking to the [Pennsylvania Capital-Star](https://www.wesanews.org/economy-business/2026-08-27/pa-food-banks-struggling-snap-cuts-economic-pain) in late August. These are the food bank's own intake numbers, not an estimate from outside.

## What SNAP Is and What Changed

SNAP is the federal grocery benefit most of us still call food stamps. In July 2025 Congress passed a law that rewrote a good part of it. Adults between 18 and 64 without a child under 14 now have to show about 20 hours a week of work, school or training, or their benefit stops after three months, as [Billy Penn laid out in October](https://billypenn.com/2026/10/06/philly-snap-benefits-changes/). The [USDA's own page on the work rules](https://www.fns.usda.gov/snap/work-requirements) describes the documentation behind them. Most noncitizens lost access, with a few exceptions such as green card holders. And everyone faces more paperwork when they apply and when they renew.

The stricter eligibility rules took effect around the start of this year. According to Bautista, that is when the 7,000 new people arrived at Share. By July, she said, around 60,000 people in Philadelphia had lost SNAP, and 30,000 new people came to Share for food that month alone.

The same Capital-Star report made a point that is easy to miss. The number of people who had lost SNAP by the end of the summer still had not reached the projections made when the law passed. In other words, the July figure was not described as a peak. It was a point on the way.

## Where the Numbers Stand Now

The picture has filled in since the summer. In October the [Philadelphia Inquirer](https://www.inquirer.com/news/snap-recipients-changes-october-2026-20261003.html) reported that almost two million Pennsylvanians were on SNAP when the law was signed, and that 234,000 of them, about 12 percent, have since fallen off the rolls. About 106,000 lost benefits because of the work requirements, 36,000 of them in Philadelphia. Children make up 29 percent of everyone who lost benefits.

Alissa Smethers, a registered dietitian and assistant professor at Temple University's College of Public Health, told Billy Penn that SNAP participation has dropped about 13 percent over the past year, partly because eligible people are not applying when they cannot gather the documents. She also pointed to what follows at the table: households on tight budgets cut fresh fruit and vegetables first and lean on cheaper packaged food.

The detail that stays with me is who those people are. Mackenzie Libbey, a supervising attorney at Community Legal Services, told the Inquirer that most of the people who have lost benefits are citizens who could not get through the paperwork. "It's just too much, and people just give up," she said.

That fits what Share's executive director, George Matysik, told [Billy Penn](https://billypenn.com/2026/10/06/philly-snap-benefits-changes/) in October. Pantries that used to see people come for a supplement now see families relying on them as the main source of food. "We were not built to be able to serve the entire Greater Philadelphia area for the folks that are food insecure," he said. In Share's 40 years, he added, the work has been supplemental, never meant to be where people get most of their food.

So the July spike did not fade into a new normal. The people who lost SNAP did not stop eating. They moved to the food bank line, and they stayed there.

## The Costs Have Moved to the State

There is a second change that lands this fall, and it is about who pays to run the program.

Since October 1, Pennsylvania pays 75 percent of what it costs to administer SNAP, up from 50 percent. Both the [Inquirer](https://www.inquirer.com/news/snap-recipients-changes-october-2026-20261003.html) and Billy Penn reported the shift, which is already in effect. That is the cost of caseworkers and systems, not the benefits themselves, and it arrives while state caseworkers are already struggling to keep up with the new paperwork.

A bigger bill comes in October 2027. From then, states with a high payment error rate will also pay part of the benefits, money the federal government has always covered. The [Central Pennsylvania Food Bank](https://www.centralpafoodbank.org/policy-blog-snap-error-rates-and-projected-cost-share-for-pennsylvania/) explains that Pennsylvania's error rate was 9.21 percent in federal fiscal year 2025, which would make the state responsible for 10 percent of benefit costs. Its own estimate puts that at around $381 million a year. The Center on Budget and Policy Priorities [estimates about $410 million](https://www.cbpp.org/blog/states-first-ever-bill-for-snap-benefits-could-cost-billions) if the 2026 error rate stays above 8 percent. The error rate mostly measures honest mistakes and missing paperwork, the food bank notes, not fraud.

Put those two changes together and the direction is plain. More people are losing benefits over paperwork, and the state that processes the paperwork is paying more to do it.

## What I Take From This

I keep coming back to one idea. The food bank line is where this policy shows up. It is not where it gets fixed.

Share Food Program, Philabundance and the pantries they supply are doing remarkable work, and they need support. But their own leaders are saying, on the record, that they were never built to replace SNAP. Every family that falls off the rolls over a missed form becomes a family the food bank now has to feed in full.

So if we want fewer new people at Share's door next July, the work starts earlier in the chain. Keep eligible families on SNAP. Make the paperwork possible to finish. Help people appeal when they are cut off by mistake; [Community Legal Services](https://clsphila.org/services/snap-food-stamps/) offers free help with exactly that. And watch the state budget, because the 2027 bill will force choices about how the program runs here.

Seven thousand in January and thirty thousand in July are not a story about one bad summer. They are a measure of how fast a policy change becomes a line outside a pantry. That is the story [I Am Hungry in Philadelphia](https://www.iamhungryinphilly.com/) exists to keep telling after the headlines move on.
