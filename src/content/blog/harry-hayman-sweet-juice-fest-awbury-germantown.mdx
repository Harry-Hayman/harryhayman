The sign at the Ardleigh Street entrance is plain about the terms. The Farm at Awbury is free and open sunrise to sunset, every day, to anyone who walks up. On Saturday, August 22, 2026, a smaller board went up beside it in handwriting, pointing the way to Sweet Juice Fest, and for one afternoon a sixteen acre working farm in Germantown became a music festival, a marketplace, an art gallery and a wellness tent at the same time. Harry Hayman went through that gate, spent the day, and came out with a single argument he has been making in different forms for years: the support that counts is the support that arrives in person.

## The Gate and the Case for Arriving

Sweet Juice Fest ran from 2:30 to 9:30 that evening on the farm at 6336 Ardleigh Street, as listed in the [Resolve Philly](https://resolvephilly.org/gih/august-2026-events-germantown/) roundup of Germantown events for August. Tickets started at a suggested minimum of ten dollars, with a suggested price of forty five, children under five admitted free, and a published email address for anyone who could not pay the minimum, per the festival's own [Sweet Juice Fest](https://luma.com/q8l7rqql) listing. The same listing states where the money goes: to the artists, to the venue, and to the comfort of the people who came.

That pricing tells the reader what kind of event this is before a single note is played. It is artist and volunteer led. There is no promoter absorbing the risk and no corporate underwriter covering a shortfall, which means the gap between a good year and a bad one is the number of people who decided, on a Saturday in August, to actually go. Harry Hayman's position is that this is where support either exists or does not. Approval expressed online costs nothing and delivers nothing to a festival that has to pay its performers at the end of the night. Attendance, and everything that follows from attendance, is the whole mechanism.

The organiser he went for is Melina Harris, a co founder of the Sweet Juice collective and its finance director, named in that role as Mel Harris in [WHYY](https://whyy.org/articles/philadelphia-germantown-sweet-juice-fest-elevating-queer-minority-artists/) reporting on the festival's return to Germantown. The collective began in 2022 and was built by a small group of friends who wanted a festival that paid attention to queer and femme musicians in Philadelphia, a history [WXPN](https://xpn.org/2024/06/12/sweet-juice-fest-noun-sam-rise/) traced when the event reached its third year. What sits behind the gate is a fifth year of that work.

## A Juice Box Full of Other People's Wishes

Near the entrance, someone had built a juice box roughly the height of a person out of cardboard, painted it orange and pink with a peach on the front and a rolled paper straw bent out of the top, and lettered it with the phrase 10% LOVE 90% REVOLUTION. A hand painted board leaned against the base with the instruction: place your dreams, wishes and secrets into the Juice Box of New Beginnings. Slips of paper and a pen sat beside it. By the end of the day the box was full.

The device is not a one off. Reporting by [Rainbow Rodeo](https://rainbowrodeomag.com/philly-sweet-juice-fest/) on the collective's 2025 edition, themed Quantum Contact, described a festival built around art, drag, dance and ritual, and around an invitation to send a message into the unknown. The through line across editions is that the audience is given something to do rather than something to watch. A festival that hands out paper and asks for a wish has decided in advance that the crowd is a participant.

Harry Hayman's reading of the full box is that community is rarely missing in Philadelphia. What is usually missing is a place to put it. Somebody has to build the container, stand it up in a field, and take the risk that nobody writes anything.

## The Music That Held the Field

The stage sat under a pavilion at the far end of the lawn, strung with warm lights, dressed at the front with a fallen log and cut flowers. A singer worked the front of it with a microphone while a keyboard player in white sat behind, and the audience was on blankets and folding chairs on the grass, close enough that nothing separated the performance from the people watching it. There was no barricade and no distance to speak of.

That closeness is deliberate and it is the festival's signature. The lineup is drawn from local acts, alongside performance and visual artists, as [PhillyVoice](https://www.phillyvoice.com/sweet-juice-fest-2024-queer-music-arts-awbury-arboretum/) described when the festival first moved onto the farm at Awbury. Philadelphia produces more working musicians than it has rooms to hold them, and a field with a pavilion, a decent sound system and a few hundred people on blankets is a genuinely good room. The music carried the day because the day was built to let it.

## Buying Something From as Many People as Possible

Two vendors ended up on Harry Hayman's camera roll. La Camelia Philly parked its truck on the gravel with the menu painted across the side in red and white, tacos and quesadillas and burritos, al pastor and birria and carne asada. A short walk away, a green canopy over two folding tables held the taps for Triple Bottom Brewing, with a member of staff pouring and a queue forming toward the food truck behind him.

Buying from as many of them as possible was the point, and not as a gesture of generosity. [Triple Bottom Brewing](https://triplebottombrewing.com/about-us) is a Philadelphia brewery on Spring Garden Street that builds living wage jobs for people who have experienced incarceration or housing insecurity, works with Project HOME and the Youth Sentencing and Reentry Project, and runs a paid apprenticeship in hospitality work. La Camelia Philly is a Mexican food truck working the same circuit of markets and festivals. Neither of them turns a profit from a festival by being admired. They turn a profit by selling out, and a vendor who sells out has a commercial reason to say yes when the same organisers call again next August. That is the difference between an event that happens twice and an event that becomes part of a neighbourhood's calendar.

## Art in a Field, Care Under a Tent

The visual art was hung outdoors with no ceremony at all. One installation used a bare branch as an armature, dressed with crepe paper blossom, a lace canopy, a small mirrored ball, a crocheted frog holding a tiny juice box and a paper mushroom, set against a painted plastic backdrop lit green. No gallery wall, no admission, no wall text explaining what the viewer was supposed to feel about it. Elsewhere a maker's table held decks of oracle cards among ribbons and flowers.

The care offered was equally unfussy. Under one canopy, Hawthorn's Brood Apothecary had laid out tinctures, salves, bath salts and herbal blends on a cloth printed with moths and beetles, under a banner naming the business as a cooperative owned by its workers. Its own site describes the work as community centred herbal support and gathering space, and the operation is listed publicly as [Hawthorn's Brood](https://www.hawthornsbrood.com/). Somewhere in the same corner of the day, somebody handed Harry Hayman a torn slip of paper with four words written on it in blue ink: walk in the grass barefoot. He did.

None of this is decorative. The festival has consistently mixed services into the programme rather than treating wellness as a separate ticketed product, offering tarot readings, haircuts from a queer barber, a clothing and art swap and a printing station in past years, as PhillyVoice documented. The idea being tested every August is that looking after yourself should be available in a field, for free, next to the beer tent.

## What the Farm Makes Possible

None of the above has anywhere to happen without the site. [The Farm at Awbury](https://awbury.org/farm/) is sixteen acres northwest of Washington Lane, shared among partners including the Awbury Community Garden, the Weavers Way Mort Brooks Farm, the Philadelphia Beekeepers Guild, the Philly Goat Project and the Pennsylvania Horticultural Society, with pedestrian entrances at 6336 Ardleigh Street and 901 East Washington Lane. It sits inside [Awbury Arboretum](https://awbury.org/), whose grounds have been free and open to the public every day for more than a century.

The land was the estate of the Cope and Haines families, Quaker shipping merchants, and the Francis Cope House at the centre of it dates to 1862, a history kept by [Historic Germantown](https://historicgermantownpa.org/awbury-arboretum/). What that inheritance produces in 2026 is a working farm of raised beds, flower gardens and stands of black eyed susans going over at the end of August, inside a public green space that costs nothing to enter.

Harry Hayman's closing observation on the day is the smallest and the most Philadelphian: the city has places like this and does not talk about them enough. Sweet Juice Fest was one Saturday. The gate is open the rest of the year.
