There is a moment in a musician's career that gets written about, and there is a longer stretch before it that does not. The first is the tour, the festival, the name on a poster somebody recognises. The second is the years of local gigs where somebody had to decide to hire an unproven player and pay them.

I have watched [Arnetta Johnson](https://www.berklee.edu/people/arnetta-johnson), the trumpeter who performs as Trumpet Chick, for long enough now to think she is the clearest case I know for working out which of those two a city can actually influence.

## Where she comes from

Johnson is from Camden, New Jersey, across the river from Philadelphia and part of the same music region. She attended the Creative and Performing Arts High School there before going on to [Berklee College of Music](https://www.berklee.edu/news/trumpet-chic-student-finds-mentorship-berklee) in Boston, where the mentorship she found became part of how she describes her own development.

The name came from other people. Audiences who could not remember what she was called referred to her as that trumpet girl, and rather than correct them she took the phrase, changed the spelling, and turned a shrug into a brand. [Philadelphia Magazine profiled her in 2019](https://www.phillymag.com/news/2019/07/13/arnetta-johnson-trumpeter/) as a player calling herself the disrupter of jazz, which is a young musician's phrase and also an accurate description of what a trumpeter does when she refuses to stay inside one genre.

In 2018 she toured internationally as part of Beyonce's On the Run II production. [WHYY has interviewed her](https://whyy.org/episodes/arnetta-johnson/) about that trajectory, and [CityWide Stories traced the route](https://citywidestories.com/2019/02/04/trumpeter-arnetta-johnson-from-camden-to-berklee-and-beyonce/) from Camden to Berklee to a stadium tour. She has also played with the Roots.

## The part that is easy to miss

A tour credit of that size reorganises how everyone reads a musician. Before it, she is a working trumpet player with a following. After it, she is somebody who played with Beyonce, and every subsequent booking is easier.

What I keep coming back to is what happened before the reorganisation, because that is the only part a city has any influence over.

Somebody in South Jersey or Philadelphia booked her when the credit was a high school and a music degree. Somebody paid her when the fee was small. Somebody put her on a bill in front of an audience that had not come to see her, and somebody else in that audience mentioned her to a third person. That sequence is invisible in a profile, and it is entirely the reason a profile eventually gets written.

By the time a national audience confirms a musician, the decisions that mattered are years behind them and were made by people whose names do not appear anywhere.

I am not arguing that early supporters deserve credit. Most of them were not being visionary; they booked a good player because they needed a good player that night. I am arguing about which decisions a region actually controls. Nobody in Philadelphia influenced whether Beyonce's musical director hired a trumpeter from Camden. A great many people in Philadelphia and South Jersey influenced whether that trumpeter was still playing professionally at twenty two, with enough work to keep her horn in her hands and her rent paid.

The second question is the one we get to answer, and we answer it in small amounts, repeatedly, mostly without ever finding out which of our bookings mattered.

## Coming home to a bigger room

I have known Johnson for years, and in late July 2026 I watched her open two nights for [Jill Scott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jill_Scott) at [The Met Philadelphia](https://www.themetphilly.com/). The Met is a restored opera house on North Broad Street that seats several thousand, and Jill Scott is a Philadelphia artist of a scale that fills it.

She is due in New York on 21 September 2026 as well, for an appearance connected with [the Gates Foundation](https://www.gatesfoundation.org/) during [United Nations General Assembly](https://www.un.org/en/ga/) week. I want to keep one distinction straight: an appearance during General Assembly week, at an event connected to a foundation, is not the same thing as appearing before the General Assembly itself. Neither engagement is on a public listing as I write this, so I am passing on the account I have rather than a confirmed date.

What makes the Met booking worth noting to me is not its size but its direction. A regional musician who has toured internationally comes back and plays one of the home city's largest rooms, opening for another artist from the same city. That is a scene functioning as a scene rather than as an export business, and it is rarer than it sounds. Plenty of cities produce musicians who leave and never play a significant local room again.

## The obligation runs earlier than the applause

I have seen the pattern often enough to state it as a rule. We support our musicians enthusiastically once a national audience has certified them, and thinly before that. The enthusiasm is genuine and it is also, in career terms, close to useless, because it arrives after the hard part.

The hard part is the years in which a musician is good but unproven, when every booking is a small risk taken by somebody who might be wrong. Those bookings are what produce the player who is eventually ready for a stadium tour, and they are made by venue owners, event organisers, band leaders and audiences, not by institutions with grant programmes.

Which means my list is short and unglamorous. Go to the shows. Hire the musician for the event rather than the playlist. Pay the fee that was quoted. Introduce them to the person who books the other room. Buy the record. Say the name to somebody who does not know it yet.

None of that requires being right about who becomes famous. It requires being willing to spend money and attention on somebody who is good now, and accepting that most of those bets will not produce a Beyonce tour, because that is not the point. The point is a region in which a talented eighteen year old can find enough paid work to keep playing.

I want to be blunt about the alternative, because it is not dramatic. Musicians who cannot make a living from music do not usually announce a departure. They take a day job that is meant to be temporary, then take fewer gigs because the day job has a schedule, then stop being available for the Thursday night that would have kept them sharp. Nobody in the city notices at any point. The player simply appears less often on bills until they stop appearing, and the loss is recorded nowhere.

## What a city gets back

The return is not primarily reputational. It is that the musicians stay, or come back, and that the next generation has somebody local to learn from.

Philadelphia and the towns around it have produced an extraordinary share of American music, and I do not think that pipeline is a natural resource. It is the accumulated effect of teachers, rooms, bandstands and people willing to hire an unknown. Every one of those is a decision somebody made, and every one of them can stop being made.

Johnson's route ran through a public arts high school, a conservatory, a lot of local gigs, and then a stage in front of tens of thousands. Only the last part is a story. The rest is infrastructure, and infrastructure is the part we can actually build.
