Two tacos in a white paper tray, corn tortillas loaded with black beans, melted cheese, guacamole, pico and red onion. Behind them, three squeeze bottles of salsa stand in a row with handwritten labels: Mild, Special, and one that just says Halloween. A bottle of tamarind Jarritos leans into the frame from the left. That is the first photo in my post this week, and honestly it explains me better than a résumé would. I'm Harry Hayman, and I've never met a taco I didn't like. At this point, I think it's hereditary. This is the longer version of that post: where the habit came from, a little about the shop in these photos, and why I think the money we spend on an ordinary weekday lunch matters more in Philadelphia than most of us give it credit for. Fair warning, it is going to make you hungry.

## Where the Habit Comes From

My dad played professional soccer in Mexico and passed down a love of Mexican food and culture. That is the whole story as I told it in the post, and it is enough. A kid who grows up with that kind of inheritance learns early that food is never only food. It carries the place it came from, and it carries the people who made it.

Years later, my friend Alejandra's dad, the photographer Guillermo Aldana, showed me around Mexico. That trip gave me a whole new appreciation for the food and the people behind it.

If you have ever looked at photographs of Mexico's traditional festivals and indigenous communities in National Geographic, Artes de México or México Desconocido, there is a decent chance you have seen his work. A [2015 profile in Noroeste](https://www.noroeste.com.mx/entretenimiento/cultura/ve-guillermo-aldana-con-el-corazon-HGNO9595) describes almost fifty years behind the camera: a start in journalism, postgraduate photography studies at Syracuse University, and a first National Geographic credit that came about when the assigned photographer fell ill on a trip to Mexcaltitlán, Nayarit, and he took the pictures himself. [Wikidata](https://www.wikidata.org/wiki/Q5854488) lists him as a Mexican photographer and historian, born in 1937.

In that same interview he says that when he arrives in an indigenous community, "lo que habla es el corazón y los ojos." What speaks is the heart and the eyes. I like that line a lot. It is also a pretty good description of how to eat in a country that is not your own. You show up, you pay attention, and you remember that every plate has a person behind it.

## Jim Rockford Had His Priorities Straight

And part of my taco education came from television. My favorite show growing up was The Rockford Files, on Friday nights.

[The Rockford Files](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Rockford_Files) ran on NBC from September 1974 to January 1980, six seasons and 123 episodes, created by Roy Huggins and Stephen J. Cannell. Jim Rockford was a Los Angeles private investigator who lived and worked out of a weathered trailer parked at the beach in Malibu, eventually at Paradise Cove. The show's star won the Emmy for lead actor in a drama series in 1977, and the series took the top drama Emmy the following year.

The part that stuck with me is small. According to the [character's own entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Rockford_%28television_character%29), Jim is frequently seen eating Mexican food, usually tacos, sometimes for breakfast. He charged a flat $200 a day plus expenses and, as a running joke, rarely collected it. So here was a working guy, one man with one trailer and a day rate he could not get anybody to pay, who always seemed to find time to stop for tacos in the middle of solving somebody else's problems. The man had his priorities straight.

I like to think Rockford knew something the rest of us take a while to learn. The good food in a city is rarely at the fanciest address. It is at the place run by people working about as hard as he was.

## The Shop in These Photos

The shop in my photos is TacoTaco Mexicano, at 261 South 44th Street near Spruce in West Philadelphia. You can see the 261 running down the red doorframe in my picture of the window. The glass carries a hand painted taco wearing a big brown sombrero, the name in yellow script, the phone number in white numerals underneath, a red and blue OPEN sign glowing in the corner, and a column of delivery app and neighborhood stickers along the left edge.

The place has a history worth knowing. [West Philly Local reported](https://www.westphillylocal.com/2019/09/03/taco-taco-restaurant-opens-at-former-honest-toms-location-near-44th-and-spruce/) in September 2019 that Taco Taco had quietly opened that August in the former Honest Tom's Plant Based Taco Shop space, with Honest Tom's longtime kitchen staff taking it over and bringing back the old menu, weekend breakfast included. [34th Street Magazine](https://www.34st.com/article/2019/09/honest-toms-closed-taco-taco-mexican-new-restaurant-university-city-spruce-hill-west-philadelphia-mexican-food) called it the next phase in the evolution of Honest Tom's and noted that the menu resembled the one from the food truck where Honest Tom's got its start. That is my favorite kind of neighborhood story. The owner moves on, and the people who actually cooked the food keep the doors open.

[Philadelphia Magazine's taco guide](https://www.phillymag.com/restaurants/best-tacos-philadelphia/) singles out exactly that weekend breakfast: potato, egg and cheese stuffed burritos and tacos every Saturday and Sunday, served on corn tortillas.

The chalkboard in my last photo is the kind of menu I trust. Rice and beans, sweet potato and veggie tacos at the top. Then walnut (marked vegan), pork, beef, chicken, carnitas, chorizo, tinga and al pastor. Birria with consome. Steak, shrimp and tilapia. A side column of chips, guac, queso, elote and nachos, and a specials board running from hibiscus jamaica and horchata to tres leches, with a yellow sign taped over one corner announcing a brisket special, tacos or burrito.

And then there is the truck. Bright yellow, wrapped in papel picado banners and cartoon tacos, with nachos, birria, tacos, quesadillas and burritos spelled out along the side and a tall TACOS flag planted out front. You do not walk past that truck by accident.

## Where Our Money Goes

So yes, support your local taco shop. And your corner bakery, your produce vendor, and the local family restaurant that knows what you order before you sit down.

I don't say that as a slogan. There is real arithmetic behind it. The research firm Civic Economics has spent years on what it calls the [Indie Impact Study Series](https://www.civiceconomics.com/indie-impact.html), surveying independent retailers and restaurants about how much of their revenue stays in the community that spends it. In Salt Lake City, the [Deseret News reported](https://www.deseret.com/2012/8/15/20505626/shopping-local-benefits-local-economy-more-new-study-says) that local independent restaurants returned nearly 79 percent of their revenue to the local economy, while national restaurants returned just over 30 percent. A Civic Economics report released in January 2023 for Dane County, Wisconsin, [covered by WisBusiness](https://www.wisbusiness.com/2023/report-highlights-impact-of-locally-owned-retailers-restaurants/), put the figure at about 61 percent for local restaurants and about 30 percent for national restaurant chains.

Those are other places, and the numbers move from city to city. The direction does not. Do the napkin math on a twenty dollar lunch. At the local spot, something like twelve to sixteen dollars of it keeps moving through the area, in wages to people who live nearby, in the owner's own spending, in the next order of supplies. At the national chain, by the same percentages, it is closer to six.

Now bring it home. The U.S. Small Business Administration's Office of Advocacy, in its [Pennsylvania small business profile](https://advocacy.sba.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Pennsylvania.pdf), counts 1.1 million small businesses in the state, 99.6 percent of all businesses, employing 2.4 million people. That is 45.4 percent of Pennsylvania's employees. Between March 2022 and March 2023, small businesses accounted for a net 89,756 new jobs, 82.7 percent of the state's net job growth. The corner places are not a nice extra on top of the economy. They are a huge share of it.

In a city where too many people are struggling to get ahead, where we spend our money matters. A sale at a neighborhood business helps pay the people who work there and gives that business a better shot at staying on the block.

## Why Harry Hayman Keeps Coming Back to the Block

There is a through line here, from my dad to a trip through Mexico with a photographer who sees with the heart and the eyes, to a fictional detective who never let a case get in the way of lunch. Food is how people make a living. It is how a block holds onto its character. And it is one of the very few civic decisions most of us make three times a day without thinking of it as one.

The shop on 44th Street is a good example of what that looks like in practice: a kitchen crew that kept a menu alive when the owner moved on, a window full of hand lettering and neighborhood stickers, a truck carrying the same name out to the curb. None of that survives on good wishes. It survives on Tuesday lunches.

So go. Take a friend. Try the walnut taco if you are curious, or the birria if you are not. Pick a salsa bottle and live with the consequences.

Anyway, now I want tacos.
