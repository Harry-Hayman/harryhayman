My flight into Chicago was delayed. So I went straight from the airport to Ever Restaurant, suitcase and all, and walked into one of the most carefully run dining rooms in the country looking exactly like a man who had just come off a delayed plane. I'm Harry Hayman, and I have spent enough years in restaurants to know what that moment can feel like from the other side of the door. A guest arrives early, or late, or carrying something nobody planned for, and the room has to decide in a few seconds what kind of place it is. The team at Ever decided without missing a beat. They took care of my luggage and offered me a place to wait. By the end of the evening I'd had a fabulous dinner, gotten to talk with Chef Curtis Duffy, and bought his memoir, Fireproof. This is the story of that night, and of why the first two minutes stayed with me longer than any single course.

## What Happened at 1340 W. Fulton Street

Ever sits at 1340 W. Fulton St. in Chicago, an address confirmed on the [MICHELIN Guide's page for Ever](https://guide.michelin.com/us/en/illinois/chicago/restaurant/ever) and on the [restaurant's own About page](https://www.ever-restaurant.com/about-ever/). I came in with my suitcase, straight from a delayed flight.

The team took care of my luggage and offered me a place to wait. Put that way it sounds small, and that is the point. They made a guest who had arrived outside the plan feel like part of it.

I want to be precise about why that mattered to me. The dinner itself was fabulous, and I will leave the courses to the people who write about food for a living. What stuck with me was how easy everyone made it feel. That starts before the first course and stays with you long after the last.

## Ever, and the Room Curtis Duffy Built

For anyone who has not been, a little context helps. [Ever's About page](https://www.ever-restaurant.com/about-ever/) describes the restaurant as the creative obsession of Chef Curtis Duffy, with an eight to ten course tasting menu over about two and a half hours, open for dinner Tuesday through Saturday by reservation only. The [Wikipedia entry on Ever](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ever_%28restaurant%29) records that it opened in July 2020, in the middle of the pandemic, and the MICHELIN Guide lists it today with two stars.

The Guide's inspectors describe the room as Venetian plaster, starry lighting and slat wall panels, with tables spaciously arranged and servers who stand at attention. You can feel all of that the moment you walk in. It is a room built for precision.

That is exactly why the suitcase moment interested me. A room like that runs on choreography. Every plate, every pour, every turn of the service is planned. A guest walking in with luggage is not in the plan. How a team handles the thing that is not in the plan tells you more about its culture than how it handles the thing that is.

## Curtis Duffy's Road to Fulton Market

Duffy did not arrive at Ever out of nowhere. Before it, he and his longtime partner ran Grace on Randolph Street in the West Loop, which earned three MICHELIN stars. [Eater Chicago reported in December 2017](https://chicago.eater.com/2017/12/21/16804212/grace-west-loop-curtis-duffy-closed-michelin-star) that Grace closed after Duffy and his general manager left and their attempt to buy the restaurant from its majority stakeholder failed. In 2016, the [Chicago Sun-Times reported](https://chicago.suntimes.com/2016/5/3/18337447/chicago-s-alinea-curtis-duffy-win-2016-james-beard-awards) that he won the James Beard Award for Best Chef, Great Lakes, for his work at Grace.

So when Ever opened in 2020, it was a second act built after losing a three star restaurant. I think that history is part of why the room feels the way it does. People who have rebuilt something tend to pay attention to the small things, because they know how easily the big things can go.

Ever also reached a much wider audience than most fine dining rooms ever do. According to the [Wikipedia summary of the episode Forks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forks_%28The_Bear%29), the second season of The Bear filmed its fine dining storyline at Ever, with Duffy cooking the meals seen on screen. In that episode a cook spends a week learning the front of house, starting by polishing forks, and comes away understanding what real care for a guest looks like. I did not expect to live a small version of that with a suitcase in tow, but there it was.

## Fireproof, the Book I Carried Home

At the end of the evening I talked with Chef Duffy and bought his memoir. The book is Fireproof: Memoir of a Chef, written with Jeremy Wagner, as listed on [Curtis Duffy's own site](https://www.curtisduffy.com/). The [Chicago Sun-Times covered its release](https://chicago.suntimes.com/food-drink/2025/07/31/curtis-duffy-book-fireproof-chicago-restaurants-ever-grace) in the summer of 2025: Dead Sky Publishing brought it out on August 5, and Wagner, a friend of Duffy's, is also one of the publisher's owners.

According to that report and to [CBS News Chicago's interview with Duffy](https://www.cbsnews.com/chicago/news/celebrity-chef-curtis-duffy-trauma-triumphs-memoir), the book goes back to the hardest part of his childhood, including the day his father killed his mother and then himself, and then follows him through Chicago's top kitchens, among them Charlie Trotter's, Alinea, Avenues and Grace. The Sun-Times piece also notes that the book covers Ever's appearance on The Bear.

I came in carrying luggage. I left carrying a book. Knowing even the outline of that story changes how I think about the man who took the time to talk with a guest who had arrived with his luggage. Some people come through what he came through and close up. He built rooms whose whole purpose is to look after strangers.

## Harry Hayman on the Guest Who Arrives Outside the Script

I have spent a long time in and around restaurants, and here is what I believe. Every restaurant has a script. The reservation, the greeting, the seating, the pacing of the courses, the check. Most guests stay inside it, and most teams are good at running it.

The real test is the guest who arrives well outside the script. The one with a suitcase. The one whose flight was late. The one who shows up early, or with an extra person, or on the worst day of their week. A team can treat that guest as a disruption to the plan, or it can treat the plan as something that exists to serve the guest. You can feel the difference in about ten seconds, and you remember it for years.

At Ever, I got the second version. The care did not feel like an exception being made for me. It felt like the normal operating posture of the place. That is the hardest thing in hospitality to build, because you cannot write it into a manual. You build it by hiring people who notice, training them to act on what they notice, and backing them when they do.

## What I Am Bringing Home to Philadelphia

Back home in Philadelphia, we talk a lot about our restaurant scene, and we should. But the thing I keep coming back to after Ever is not the food or the plates. It is that moment at the door. It does not take a tasting menu or a MICHELIN star to make a guest with a suitcase feel welcome. Any neighborhood spot can do it. It takes a team that sees a person instead of a problem.

This dinner happened on the same Chicago trip as my visit to the Obama Presidential Center, which I wrote about in [my post on the Obama Presidential Center](https://harryhayman.com/blog/obama-presidential-center-chicago-visit). That piece was about a place built for a whole city. This one is about something much smaller, one team, one evening and one tired guest, and I think the two lessons sit well side by side.

Chef Duffy and team, thank you. I came in carrying luggage. I left carrying a book and thinking about how good you are at what you do.
