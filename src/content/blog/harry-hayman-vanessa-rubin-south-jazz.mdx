The jazz vocalist [Vanessa Rubin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanessa_Rubin) played [SOUTH Restaurant and Jazz Club](https://www.southjazzkitchen.com/) on North Broad Street across the weekend of 21 to 23 August 2026, six sets over three nights. She brought Brandon McCune on piano, [Kenny Davis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenny_Davis_(musician)) on bass and Jerome Jennings on drums.

Our room holds a few hundred people. I have come to think that number is the whole point, and I want to be specific about why.

## Who she is

Rubin is from Cleveland and has spent more than forty years inside this music. The credits I care about are not the famous ones. She worked in the [Loud Minority Band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Foster_(musician)), the big band led by the Count Basie tenor player and arranger who later took over that orchestra. Singing in a band that size is where a vocalist learns to hold a line against the horns rather than in front of them. She studied with [Barry Harris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_Harris), the pianist whose Jazz Cultural Theater in New York functioned as an informal conservatory for a generation of players. She has portrayed [Billie Holiday](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Billie_Holiday) on the theatrical stage, which is a different discipline again.

Her most recent album is The Dream Is You: Vanessa Rubin Sings Tadd Dameron, the first full length vocal tribute to [Tadd Dameron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tadd_Dameron), the most prominent composer and arranger of the bebop era and, like Rubin, a Clevelander. For that record she went to people who had actually worked with Dameron for the arrangements, [Benny Golson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benny_Golson) among them. [The Arts Fuse reviewed it](https://artsfuse.org/180692/jazz-cd-review-vanessa-rubin-sings-tadd-dameron-paying-expert-homage/) as expert homage rather than a covers exercise, and I think that is exactly the right distinction. The album was nominated for an NAACP Image Award for outstanding jazz album.

[WRTI](https://www.wrti.org/) put her in its weekly spotlight ahead of the Philadelphia dates, alongside listings for the Aidan McKeon Quartet and the Ken Fowser Quartet. Nate Chinen, the station's editorial director, wrote up the run in [Moment's Notice](https://www.wrti.org/wrti-spotlight/2026-08-16/vanessa-rubin-aidan-mckeon-and-the-ken-fowser-quartet), describing a jazz vocal tradition that values poise as much as prowess.

## The argument for a small room

There is a version of live music built for scale. The performer is a distant figure, the sound is engineered to travel, and most of the audience experiences the event through a phone screen held above the head in front of them. That version has its place and it pays for a great deal of touring, and I am not against it.

It also loses something specific, and I want to be clear that the loss is not sentimental. It is technical.

Jazz is an argument between musicians conducted in real time. The bassist decides where the beat sits and the drummer either agrees or pushes back. The pianist leaves a hole and the singer decides whether to fill it. Those decisions are made in glances and in eighth notes, and they are the actual content of the performance. In a room where the audience can see a bass player's hands and hear a singer take a breath before a phrase, the audience is watching the decisions get made. In a room built for four thousand people, they are watching the result.

That is why I do not treat the size of our room as a marketing question. A few hundred seats is not a smaller version of an arena. It is a different instrument.

## Why rooms like this are fragile

Philadelphia produces an extraordinary quantity of musicians relative to the number of rooms that can pay them. [Jazz Philadelphia](https://jazzphiladelphia.org/for-musicians/our-scene/) maps a scene of venues, educators and working players that is deep in talent and thin in stages.

Rooms close for ordinary reasons. A lease renews at a number that no longer works. A neighbourhood changes and the audience that used to walk there now drives somewhere else. An owner retires and nobody takes it on. None of those are dramatic and none of them make the news until the room is already gone.

The economics are unforgiving in a way arena touring is not, and I can tell you that from the inside. A venue that seats a few hundred cannot make the numbers work on ticket price alone. It works when the audience also eats and drinks, which is why the serious small jazz rooms in this country are almost all restaurants that happen to have a stage rather than concert halls that happen to have a bar. We built SOUTH that way on purpose: a dining room, a bar and a sound proofed jazz parlour, each doing its own job, with [a kitchen](https://www.southjazzkitchen.com/menus/) I expect to hold up its end.

Across that August weekend our kitchen ran a limited editions menu against the run: a Southern Peach Ginger Chicken, a crab and tomato chopped salad, a Blackberry Bourbon Sangria at the bar. None of that is incidental to the music. It is the mechanism that lets the music happen at that scale in that room.

Which means that when you book a table and order dinner, you are not consuming. You are the funding model. A full room on a Friday is what makes it possible to book a singer of Rubin's standing into a space where four hundred people can hear her breathe, and an empty one is how a city loses that option.

## What was actually on the stage

Four musicians, no click track, no backing tracks, no second chances. McCune, Davis and Jennings are working professionals with their own careers, hired for a weekend and expected to build something with a singer in front of an audience that paid to watch it happen.

Every set was different from the one before it. I am not making a claim about quality there, I am describing the form. The same four people playing the same songs on Friday and on Saturday produce two different performances, because the audience is different and the room is different and somebody makes a choice at bar nine that nobody made the night before.

Then it is over, and it does not exist anywhere. There is no recording of most of what happens in rooms like this. The people who were there are the archive.

That is a strange thing to build a cultural sector on, and I would rather name it plainly than romanticise it. Almost everything else a city funds leaves an object behind. A building stands up. A book stays on a shelf. A mural is still on the wall in ten years and can be photographed, catalogued and pointed at when somebody asks what the money bought.

A set of live music leaves nothing except the people who heard it and whatever it did to them. That makes it extremely hard to justify in the language institutions normally use, and I suspect that is why small venues stay chronically underfunded relative to their importance. There is no artefact to put in the annual report.

So here is my case for going. Not that live music is more authentic than recorded music, which is a tired argument I have no interest in having, but that a particular performance in a particular room on a particular night is available exactly once, and the room where it happened stays open only if enough of us decide that is worth a Friday.
