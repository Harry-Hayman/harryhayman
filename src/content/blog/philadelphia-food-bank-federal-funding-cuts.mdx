Thirty six thousand Philadelphians have lost their food stamps under the new SNAP work rules, the Philadelphia Inquirer [reported in October 2026](https://www.inquirer.com/news/snap-recipients-changes-october-2026-20261003.html). SNAP is the federal grocery benefit most of us still call food stamps. Under the federal law signed in the summer of 2025, most adults without young children now have to show about 20 hours a week of work, school, training or volunteering, or the benefit stops after three months. Statewide, about 106,000 Pennsylvanians have lost SNAP because of those work requirements, and about 234,000, roughly 12 percent of the people who were on it, have come off the rolls for every reason since the law was signed. When someone loses SNAP, the advice they get is almost always the same: go to a food bank. Food banks do exist for exactly this. Philabundance, the region's largest hunger relief organization, has spent four decades doing precisely that work. But a food bank can only hand out what it has, and in the same stretch that Washington cut SNAP, it also cut the federal food that stocks Philabundance's own shelves. That is the part of this story most people never hear.

## The Sentence I Keep Hearing

Every time I write about SNAP, someone repeats a version of the same reassurance. The federal government trims the program, but food banks exist for exactly this kind of moment, so the neediest households will not actually go without. I understand why people reach for it. It is a genuinely comforting idea, and it is not entirely wrong. [Philabundance](https://www.philabundance.org) serves roughly ninety thousand people a week across the Delaware Valley, and it has absorbed federal retreats before without collapsing. I am not writing this to argue that food banks should stop trying, or that the people who run them are doing the work poorly. They are doing it well. What I am arguing is that the sentence treats the backup system as though it operates on its own separate supply, immune to whatever happens to the main one. It does not. Philabundance buys and receives a meaningful share of what it distributes through federal channels, and those channels are being cut on the same calendar as SNAP itself, not on some safer, later one that gives anyone time to adjust.

## What Philabundance Is Actually Losing

Here is the number that changed how I read this story. Philabundance expects to receive about 62 percent less food from the federal government across 2026 and 2027 than it received in 2024, according to [PhillyVoice's reporting on the organization's own planning figures](https://www.phillyvoice.com/philabundance-federal-food-program-snap-cuts/). That is not a worst case number somebody floated in a meeting. It is the figure Philabundance is using to plan its own operations for the next two years, which means it is already built into how much food shows up at pantries across Philadelphia, Bucks, Chester, Delaware and Montgomery counties, and into how much does not. The reduction traces to two decisions inside the United States Department of Agriculture. The Emergency Food Assistance Program, the long running channel that sends commodity food to food banks nationwide, was scaled back starting in March 2025. And the [Local Food Purchase Assistance Cooperative Agreement program](https://www.ams.usda.gov/selling-food-to-usda/lfpacap), a newer and more targeted stream, was eliminated entirely that same month.

## The Program That Bought From Pennsylvania Farms

The Local Food Purchase Assistance program is worth slowing down on, because it was never just another line item. It paid food banks to buy fresh produce, dairy and meat directly from local growers, the category of food that is hardest to source through donations and the first to disappear when a supply chain tightens. Since the program began in 2022, Philabundance had purchased more than four million dollars in food from Pennsylvania growers through it, according to the same PhillyVoice reporting. Losing that program took roughly one and a half million dollars out of Philabundance's budget. And it came on top of the bigger cut: the [62 percent drop in federal food](https://www.phillyvoice.com/philabundance-federal-food-program-snap-cuts/) the organization expects in 2026 and 2027, which its chief food sourcing officer, Lauren Webb, told PhillyVoice will reduce its purchasing power by 18 percent in 2026. Sit with that number for a second. It is not 18 percent of one grant among many. It is 18 percent of what the organization can afford to buy. And the need is moving the other way: Pennsylvania's 18 food banks have seen need rise about 40 percent over the last two years, Share Food Program's George Matysik [told Billy Penn](https://billypenn.com/2026/05/18/snap-cuts-philly-food-banks-strained/). The [USDA's own program page](https://www.ams.usda.gov/selling-food-to-usda/lfpacap) describes the Local Food Purchase Assistance Cooperative Agreement as funding meant to strengthen local and regional food supply chains and support local producers at the same time it feeds people. Philadelphia was drawing both benefits from the same dollars, and both ended together, on the same date.

## Two Cuts, One Bill Season

This is the part of the story that turns a budget problem into an argument. SNAP and the Local Food Purchase Assistance program are administered differently, funded through different mechanisms, and covered in the news as though they are unrelated stories. [Reporting from the Food Research and Action Center](https://frac.org/news/agricultureappropriationsbillapr2026) and the [Center on Budget and Policy Priorities](https://www.cbpp.org/research/food-assistance/snap-tracker-people-are-losing-food-assistance-as-the-republican-megabill) both trace the SNAP reductions to the same 2025 congressional session that produced the agricultural funding decisions ending programs like LFPA. Lining up a food bank's federal supply against the SNAP numbers on the same page is the first thing I check when I read a food economy budget, because that is where the real capacity question actually lives, not in either number by itself. When I do that here, the coincidence stops looking like one. The main system and the backup meant to catch what falls out of it were both reduced inside the same twelve month stretch, by the same set of federal decisions, without anyone coordinating whether the second system could absorb what the first one dropped.

## What Philabundance Itself Is Saying

I do not want to make this argument from a spreadsheet alone, so it is worth hearing how Philabundance describes it in its own words. [Coverage from CBS News Philadelphia](https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/philadelphia/news/pennsylvania-food-banks-farmers-philabundance-usda-funding) reported that Pennsylvania food banks and the farmers who supply them were left scrambling once USDA ended the program. Philabundance's chief food sourcing officer, Lauren Webb, has put the next twelve months of additional loss at roughly two million more dollars in food that will not arrive, on top of the half million dollars already gone since last spring. Philabundance's chief executive, Loree D. Jones, has been direct about what that means in practice. She has said plainly that the organization cannot simply make up losses at this scale on its own, no matter how well it fundraises or how efficiently it operates. I take her at her word, because the math backs her up. A food bank can get more efficient. It cannot make eighteen percent of its purchasing power reappear through better logistics alone.

## Ask Who Is Paying for the Food

So here is where I land, as someone who moves between kitchens, pantries and policy meetings across this city for a living. The advice to go to a food bank is not wrong. It describes a real system, staffed by people who show up for exactly this kind of moment, including at Philabundance and across the wider [Feeding America network](https://www.feedingamerica.org/research/map-the-meal-gap/by-county) it belongs to. But a sentence that treats the backup as permanently reliable, immune to the same political weather that hits the main program, is not describing 2026. It is describing an assumption left over from a calmer year. Before anyone repeats that line to reassure a neighbor that things will be fine, I would ask them to do the same thing I do for a living. Check what the food bank was actually handed. In this bill season, in this city, it was handed a great deal less than it had the year before, and the people who run it have said so, in public, on the record. That is not a reason to give up on food banks. It is a reason to stop treating them as a system that can absorb cuts for free.
