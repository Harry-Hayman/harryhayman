This is the month the salad moves. Around now the leafy greens in a Philadelphia supermarket stop coming from the coast of central California and start coming from the desert, from the farms around Yuma, Arizona, and across the line in California's Imperial County. According to the [Yuma Fresh Vegetable Association](https://www.yumafreshveg.com/yuma-ag-water/), the area grows about 90 percent of the leafy greens eaten in the United States and Canada during the winter months. That is a trade group's number, so I checked it against the government's. The USDA's Economic Research Service puts it this way in a [chart on where lettuce comes from](https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/charts-of-note/106516): from mid-November through early April, most lettuce sold in the United States is grown in the irrigated desert valleys of Imperial County and the Yuma area. Same story, plainer words. For about five months a year, one small corner of the desert is the salad bowl of a continent. And this year the river that waters it took federal cuts.

## What the Federal Government Ordered in August

The seven states that share the Colorado River spent years trying to agree on how to divide a river that keeps shrinking, and they did not get there. So on 21 August 2026 the Department of the Interior set the rules itself. Under the two-year plan, [Arizona, California and Nevada must together cut their use of river water by 1.25 million acre-feet a year in 2027 and 2028](https://www.azcentral.com/story/news/local/arizona-environment/2026/08/21/colorado-river-plan-arizona/91405876007/). Arizona takes the largest share at 760,000 acre-feet, California 440,000 and Nevada 50,000, as [ABC News reported the same day](https://abcnews.com/US/federal-government-orders-steep-colorado-river-water-cuts/story?id=135856655).

An acre-foot is the water it takes to cover an acre one foot deep. Cronkite News, in its [10 September report on what the cuts mean for Arizona farms](https://cronkitenews.azpbs.org/2026/09/10/water-cuts-arizona-farms/), notes that 760,000 acre-feet could supply Phoenix for about four years. It also reports that an earlier draft, floated in January, called for Lower Basin cuts of up to 3 million acre-feet, which could have taken up to 77 percent of Arizona's share. Nevada has already gone to court to block the August order.

## Why Yuma Was Spared, and For How Long

Here is the part that surprised me. The farms that grow the winter salad were not hit, at least not yet. The same Cronkite report explains why: Yuma area farms hold water rights that are senior to almost everyone else's in Arizona, so the cuts land first on the Central Arizona Project, the canal system that serves most of the state's people.

Spared is not the same as safe. The Bureau of Reclamation will revisit the allocations after next winter. Tom Buschatzke, director of the Arizona Department of Water Resources, told Cronkite that if the drought continues and Reclamation strictly enforces water rights by seniority, Yuma farmers could lose about 30 percent of their river water. He called that outcome "draconian." It is a scenario, not a forecast, and I want to be careful with it. But it is a scenario described by the man who runs Arizona's water, about the farms that grow most of our winter lettuce.

The farmers themselves are not sitting still. John Boelts, who farms 3,000 acres around Yuma and leads the Arizona Farm Bureau, told Cronkite that growers have spent years leveling land, reshaping beds and upgrading irrigation to use less water. "We know that there's not an infinite supply," he said. I take that seriously. This is not a story about bad farming. Yuma is very good at what it does. That is exactly why so much depends on it.

## This Is About Concentration, Not Drought

I am not predicting empty shelves in January. Nobody serious is. My point is narrower and, I think, more useful: when one region grows almost all of something for five months, any bad week in that region becomes everyone's bad week. We have already seen what that looks like, twice.

The first time was about safety. In 2018, [the CDC traced an E. coli O157:H7 outbreak to romaine lettuce from the Yuma growing region](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/ecoli/2018/o157h7-04-18/index.html). Two hundred and ten people in 36 states got sick, 96 were hospitalized and five died. Investigators found the outbreak strain in canal water samples from the area. One local problem, felt in 36 states.

The second time was this year, and it was about weather. In its [April 2026 Vegetables and Pulses Outlook](https://www.ers.usda.gov/media/20874/vgs-378.pdf), the Economic Research Service reported that during February 2026, domestic shipping point prices rose for broccoli, cauliflower, celery and romaine lettuce because unseasonably high temperatures in Western Arizona and Southern California lowered yields and reduced supply. A few warm weeks in one desert, and the price moved for the whole country.

Water is the third pressure on the same place. None of the three is a catastrophe on its own. Together they make the case that a winter salad with a single address is a fragile thing.

## What I Am Not Saying About Indoor Farms

I run Concrete Greens, so you might expect me to say an indoor farm fixes the Colorado River. It does not, and I will not pretend it does. Most of the river's farm water does not go to lettuce, and a warehouse of greens in Philadelphia does not put a drop back in Lake Mead. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

What a local indoor farm can do is smaller and more honest. It can be a second source. Leafy greens are one of the few crops that grow well indoors, under lights, close to the people who eat them, every month of the year. A city that grows even a share of its own winter greens has somewhere else to turn when the desert has a hot February, a recall or a dry year. That is not a replacement for Yuma. It is a spare tire.

I wrote about one version of this earlier this month, the Amsterdam farm that [put its salad in about 1,100 Dutch supermarkets by pricing it like the bag beside it](https://harryhayman.com/blog/growy-amsterdam-vertical-farm-1100-dutch-supermarkets). The lesson there was discipline: a real buyer, a normal price, a range kept small. The lesson here is about why a city would want that farm in the first place.

## The Philadelphia Question

So here is what I keep asking, and what I would like more people in this city to ask. Where does a Philadelphia January salad grow? For most of us, the honest answer is somewhere around Yuma, on the other side of the country, watered by a river under federal cuts.

The second question matters more. What is the second source? If the answer is "there isn't one," that is worth knowing before a bad winter, not during one. A school district buying salad for lunch trays, a hospital kitchen, a corner store that wants fresh greens on the shelf in February: each of them depends on a supply chain they did not design and cannot see.

That is the gap Concrete Greens is built for. Local greens all year, sold at a price people already pay, with costs covered and the surplus going to neighbors who need it. Not instead of Yuma. Alongside it.

Here is my ask. Next time you buy a bag of salad this winter, turn it over and read where it was grown. Then ask the store, the school or the kitchen you rely on one simple question: if that place had a bad month, where would the greens come from instead? If nobody knows, that is the conversation worth starting.
