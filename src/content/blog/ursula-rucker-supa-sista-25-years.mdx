Philadelphia in the 1990s was not quiet. I was moving through a scene that had not yet figured out what to call itself, and I liked it that way. The jazz clubs stayed open late. The poets showed up early. The musicians and the spoken word artists and the producers were all in the same room, often arguing, sometimes collaborating, occasionally making something that nobody had a category for yet. [Zanzibar Blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zanzibar_Blue), the room the Bynum brothers built first on 11th Street between Spruce and Pine and later at the Bellevue on Broad Street, was one of those rooms. A lot of things started there. And one of the voices that came out of it, in those years, was Ursula Rucker.

I ran into her recently, and the conversation turned to [Supa Sista](https://www.discogs.com/master/117184-Ursula-Rucker-Supa-Sista), her debut album. It came out in 2001. That makes this year the 25th anniversary, which is a fact I already knew in some part of my brain but had not yet sat with fully. She is still doing the work. She is still performing, still writing, still pushing. I found myself thinking that this is exactly the kind of thing that deserves more than a footnote in someone else's anniversary recap. So I am writing it myself.

## What Zanzibar Blue Was and Why Any of This Begins There

Zanzibar Blue opened in 1990 and ran for seventeen years, first in a storefront space on 11th Street and later in a larger room at the Bellevue Hotel on South Broad Street. The Bynum brothers, Robert and Benjamin, built it as a jazz club, and they meant that seriously. This was not ambient music for people eating dinner. This was a room where jazz happened every night with the full weight of the tradition behind it. The club earned its reputation not just from the names it brought in, Lou Rawls, Chick Corea, Chuck Mangione, but from the consistency of what it offered. You could go there and expect something real.

What made it more than a performance venue, at least in those years, was the way it functioned as a gathering point for the broader arts community. Philadelphia in the early 1990s was a city where things were happening across disciplines and across neighborhoods, and Zanzibar Blue was one of the places where those things collided in the same room on the same night. Poets were there. Producers were there. The musicians heard the poets and the poets heard the musicians and the whole thing developed its own internal logic.

Ursula Rucker read publicly at Zanzibar Blue for the first time in 1994. She had been writing poetry since adolescence, keeping it largely to herself, and when she finally took the stage the room responded. I know what those nights felt like. I know how rarely a voice comes along that makes the musicians in the room put down whatever conversation they were having and actually listen. She had something specific to say and she had found a way to say it that was entirely her own, rooted in Philadelphia and in her own life and absolutely uninterested in making itself smaller for a more comfortable audience.

She had studied journalism at [Temple University](https://www.temple.edu/), and that background shaped the precision of her work. Journalism teaches you to be accountable to facts, to make claims you can stand behind, to say what you mean without ornamentation that obscures. She brought that discipline to poetry. The poem was not a performance for its own sake. It was a form of reporting.

## Supa Sista and the Album That Arrived on Its Own Terms

[Supa Sista](https://www.discogs.com/master/117184-Ursula-Rucker-Supa-Sista) came out in 2001 on [!K7 Records](https://k7.com/), a label based in Berlin that had built a reputation for releasing work that did not fit anywhere else. That fit was not accidental. The album moved between jazz and hip hop production and spoken word with a fluency that made those categories feel like arbitrary lines someone had drawn on a map that the music itself was not consulting. It was a debut that did not announce itself tentatively. It made its argument and waited for the listener to catch up.

I want to be honest about what it means to have been around in Philadelphia during the years that produced this record. Some of what I witnessed was by design. A lot of it was because I was in the right place at the right time in a city that rewards that kind of attention. The scene that Supa Sista emerged from was something real. The record did not come out of nowhere. It came out of years of work, of rooms like Zanzibar Blue, of a specific Philadelphia creative moment that was building pressure the way those moments do before they produce something you can actually hold.

The album had a 25th anniversary this year, and the date of that release, September 4, 2001, happens to land today. Some anniversaries you mark because they feel distant. This one does not feel distant at all.

## The Roots and What Philadelphia Makes When It Has the Room to Do It

Before Supa Sista, Ursula had already been heard on records that were circulating widely and being studied seriously. [The Roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ursula_Rucker) called on her when they needed a poet for their 1995 debut, [Do You Want More?!!!??!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Do_You_Want_More%3F!!!%3F%3F!). The spoken word piece she contributed to close that album became one of the record's most discussed passages. When they made [Illadelph Halflife](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illadelph_Halflife) in 1996, she was there again. And then on [Things Fall Apart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Things_Fall_Apart_(The_Roots_album)) in 1999, one of the most regarded records the band ever produced, she delivered "The Return to Innocence Lost," a poem she has said she wrote largely on a plane while pregnant with her second son. The piece is about addiction and fathers and the way a life can go sideways from a beginning it did not choose. It is not comfortable listening. It was not meant to be.

The Roots and Ursula Rucker together tell a specific Philadelphia story. This city makes things by putting the right people in the same physical space for long enough that they start shaping each other's work. The musicians and the poets were not operating in separate lanes that occasionally intersected. They were part of the same ongoing conversation, going to the same rooms, absorbing the same influences, building a shared vocabulary without any formal agreement that they were doing so. The four albums on which Ursula's voice appears were Philadelphia making itself audible to the rest of the country on its own terms.

## What Came After, and the Recognition That Followed

After Supa Sista, [Ursula kept building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ursula_Rucker). Silver or Lead arrived in 2003. Ma'at Mama in 2006. Ruckus Soundsysdom in 2008. She Said in 2011. Each album extended the project she had announced in 2001, pushing further into territory she had not fully mapped on the previous record. She was not trying to reproduce the debut. She was continuing an argument across years, across collaborations, across whatever the form required.

The formal recognition came, as it tends to in this city, from people who pay sustained attention. The [Leeway Foundation](https://www.leeway.org/grantees/ursula-rucker), which funds artists whose work is rooted in social change, gave her their Transformation Award. In 2018, the [Pew Center for Arts and Heritage](https://www.pewcenterarts.org/fellow/ursula-rucker) named her a Pew Fellow. The Pew Fellowship is Philadelphia's own acknowledgment that a body of work has reached a level of sustained importance, awarded by people who have spent years watching what the city actually produces. It is not an honorary degree. It is earned.

None of this surprised anyone who had been paying attention since the Zanzibar Blue years. The question is never whether the work is serious. The question is whether the institutions catch up to it in time.

## What Harry Hayman Is Doing About This Anniversary

When I ran into Ursula and we talked about where 25 years had gone, I started thinking about what it means to watch someone build a body of work across that span of time and still be in it, still performing, still going places on the page that she has not been before. [Philadelphia Magazine called her phenomenal](https://www.phillymag.com/things-to-do/2015/11/04/9-questions-with-the-phenomenal-ursula-rucker/) in a profile years ago, which is one of those words that people reach for when the more specific ones seem insufficient. [WHYY wrote about the way poetry functioned as salvation for her](https://whyy.org/articles/philadelphia-poet-finds-salvation-in-poetry/), the way it gave her a form for saying what needed to be said and surviving the saying of it. That is not a small claim. That is what art is actually for when it is working.

We are putting together a celebration. Intimate, Philadelphia, something that tries to do justice to what she has put into this city over 25 years. The date is not set. The venue is not locked in. We are working on it, and when it is ready you will know.

The phrase I keep coming back to is one I believe in: give people their flowers while they are standing there to smell them. Ursula Rucker has been standing in Philadelphia, building something serious, for 25 years. This is not a retrospective. She is not done. This is a right now acknowledgment of a living body of work.

I was in the room at Zanzibar Blue when some of it was starting. Not always by design. Often because I was paying enough attention to be somewhere worth being. September of 2026, 25 years after that album landed, I am still here and so is she. We are going to make something of that.
