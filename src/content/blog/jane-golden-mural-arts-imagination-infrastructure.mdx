Somebody handed Jane Golden a canvas that evening. It was painted in graffiti style, her name rendered in the same kind of letterforms she had spent four decades rescuing from walls across Philadelphia and turning into something the city could point to with pride. She held it, looked at it, and then spoke into a microphone while an outdoor crowd went quiet around her.

"We believed in the possibility of change," she said, "and that has power."

I had the privilege of being at that gathering, and I have not stopped thinking about that sentence since I walked away. Not because it was the most eloquent line of the evening, though it was close. Because she said it the way people say things they have already proved. Not as aspiration. As evidence.

## What Jane Golden Built

[Mural Arts Philadelphia](https://muralarts.org/) is today the largest public art program in the United States. The organization traces its roots to 1984, when Philadelphia Mayor Wilson Goode launched the [Philadelphia Anti-Graffiti Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philadelphia_Anti-Graffiti_Network) to address graffiti spreading across the city. Jane Golden, working with the program at the time, proposed something that must have sounded improbable: instead of prosecuting graffiti writers, give them legal walls. Channel what the city was trying to eradicate into something it could actually display. The Mural Arts Program formally launched in 1986 under her direction.

Her first project was a mural on the Spring Garden Street Bridge, completed with young people from the Mantua neighborhood. The city kept her on full time after that. She stayed for four decades.

When she announced in August 2025 that she would step down as Executive Director in July 2026, the news stopped people in their scrolling. [WHYY covered the announcement](https://whyy.org/articles/mural-arts-philadelphia-founding-director-steps-down/) and noted that by then, the organization she built had produced more than 4,000 works of public art and employed approximately 6,000 artists over its history. [According to the Jane Golden biography on the Mural Arts site](https://muralarts.org/artists/jane-golden/), she holds a master of fine arts from Rutgers University and undergraduate degrees in both fine arts and political science from Stanford, along with honorary doctorates from eight institutions including Swarthmore College, the University of the Arts, and Villanova University. None of that is why Philadelphia owes her what it owes her. The walls are.

## From the Anti-Graffiti Network to Four Thousand Walls

The math behind what Mural Arts Philadelphia accomplished is genuinely hard to hold in one thought. [According to the Mural Arts Philadelphia Wikipedia entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mural_Arts_Philadelphia), the program runs between fifty and one hundred public art projects each year. Roughly 2,000 murals remain publicly viewable across the city today, which is what led organizations like Visit Philadelphia to call this city home to the world's largest outdoor art gallery. In a single stretch from 2001 to 2004, the program produced more than 600 murals across Philadelphia's neighborhoods. Since the beginning, the total has exceeded 4,000.

Jane said that evening that Mural Arts had ridden imagination "fearlessly, tenaciously, in an unstoppable way." She said those thousands of artists and community members had "defied expectations repeatedly" and proved "that art could change the way people see their lives and the city."

That is a process description, not a slogan. Show up in a neighborhood. Listen to what the people there actually want to see on their walls. Then make it real. Repeat this for four decades across an entire city and the results speak clearly enough.

She also told us something that landed differently as the evening went on. In 1998, she wrote an essay where she described Philadelphia's murals as the city's "dreams manifest." She quoted that line herself and called it as true now as it was then. The word she used was "ambitious," not past tense. She was still proud of it. I think she earned that.

## Art as a City Service

The [City of Philadelphia's Mural Arts department page](https://www.phila.gov/departments/mural-arts-philadelphia/) describes the work as a partnership across community organizations, city agencies, schools, and the private sector. That is accurate and also somewhat sterile for what actually happens. Mural Arts has worked inside correctional facilities, mental health programs, and schools not as an addition but as a core service. The Porch Light program addresses mental illness and addiction through art. The Healing Walls program brings together incarcerated people, crime victims, and community members in facilitated dialogue. The biography section on the Mural Arts site describes these as rigorous initiatives in youth education, restorative justice, and behavioral health. In 1991, the Ash Center at Harvard's Kennedy School gave the Philadelphia program its Innovation in American Government Award, which is not a prize handed to decoration.

Jane described the organization that evening as completely undaunted from the beginning, dedicated to delivering art as a literal city service, with a commitment that never softened even when the skeptics were loudest. The skeptics were loud. She talked about being told, repeatedly, that murals were decoration, that art could not change neighborhoods, could not heal, could not create peace, could not connect people separated by distances that seemed impossible to bridge. Every time, the response from her and the artists she worked with was the same: why not? Then they went and did it again.

"Together, we have defied expectations repeatedly," she said. "We prove that art could change the way people see their lives and the city."

## Imagination Is Infrastructure

Here is the idea I took away from that evening and cannot put down. Jane said: "Sometimes I think what is lacking in our world... is imagination, because there's such power in that, and Mural Arts has been able to ride it and ride it and ride it fearlessly, tenaciously, in an unstoppable way."

Imagination, in this framing, is not softness. It is not decoration or distraction or an arts and crafts concept. It is the capacity to see the city that does not yet exist and then have the nerve to actually build it. Most of the time the obstacle is not resources or political will or logistics alone. It is the failure to believe that the city you can picture could actually be made real.

She said something else that evening about what she believes public life is missing right now. She did not name a policy or a funding program. She described a quality of commitment, the kind that says with full conviction: we are going to do this. No hedging. No lengthy debate about all the reasons it might not work. Just the decision to see it through, completely. Her four decades were the clearest possible evidence that this kind of commitment, held consistently and passed on to thousands of artists and community members over time, produces actual results.

She said: "We have shown that art can create connection where there was incredible distance... that art, yes art, can really create peace, and that it can give people a voice, and it can heal."

And then: "It can tell stories that have long been overlooked and it can shine a brilliant, unstoppable light on people's stories and lives, their journeys, their struggles, their triumphs."

Those are not aspirational claims about what art might do one day. In Philadelphia, they are documented history. Four thousand documented instances of it.

## The 2026 Moment and What Comes Next

Jane is not leaving at a quiet moment. [Mural Arts Philadelphia has seven major projects tied to the Semiquincentennial](https://muralarts.org/stories/seven-semiquincentennial-projects-coming-in-2026/), the national celebration of America's 250th anniversary. Printmaking by the People, a collaboration with the Free Library of Philadelphia asking what a contemporary Declaration of Independence might say, has engaged more than 1,000 participants across fifty workshops since 2024. The 52 Weeks of Firsts initiative has placed sculptures by 25 local artists across Philadelphia's neighborhoods. Throughlines, a river to river exhibition this summer, traces themes of democracy through public art installations spread across the city. She is stepping away into the most watched year Philadelphia has seen in a generation, with fifteen major projects carrying her organization across the finish line.

[The Philadelphia Inquirer's reporting on her departure](https://www.inquirer.com/arts/jane-golden-mural-arts-stepping-down-20250825.html) noted that she will remain involved as an ambassador while the organization transitions, and that the search for her successor began in fall 2025. What she is leaving behind is not a gap. It is a methodology: show up, listen, imagine, make the thing real, even when everyone around you is patiently explaining why it cannot be done. That methodology is in the walls. It is also in the thousands of artists and community members she trained to believe the same thing.

That canvas with her name on it, painted in graffiti style by the city she spent four decades rebuilding wall by wall, was the right gift. It named what she actually did. She took what the city was afraid of and turned it into what the city is recognized for everywhere. The question now is who picks up from there, and whether the rest of Philadelphia can stay, as she put it, completely undaunted about what still needs to be built.

"Thank you for taking risks, for listening, for imagining," she told the artists gathered in that outdoor space. "For bringing your talent and your humanity into communities."

I believe she was also talking to the rest of us.
