The room on North Broad Street fills twice for this one, at [6:30 and 9:00 on Thursday, September 10](https://www.southjazzkitchen.com/event/coltrane-unscripted-featuring-gerald-veasley-with-jaleel-shaw-adam-faulk-khary-shaheed-thursday-september-10-630pm-900pm-presented-by-unscripted-jazz/), for a show that will not have a set list in any ordinary sense. Coltrane Unscripted is back at SOUTH, and every time this title lands on our calendar I feel a charge I don't get from most bookings. It isn't because Gerald Veasley, Jaleel Shaw, Adam Faulk and Khary Abdul Shaheed are going to play John Coltrane's songs the way he recorded them. They aren't. They're going to walk into a seventy five seat room and do something closer to what Coltrane spent his whole career doing: listen hard, take a real risk, and follow the first true idea of the night wherever it leads. I've watched enough of these nights to know they never sound the same twice. That unpredictability is the entire point.

## What "Unscripted" Actually Means

I want to be specific about what this show is not, because the title gets misread. This is not a tribute act. Nobody up there is going to reproduce a Coltrane solo phrase for phrase or hand the crowd a note perfect recreation of something that already exists on record. That would be an impersonation, and Coltrane himself spent almost no time standing still long enough to be impersonated. He moved from bebop to modal playing to the searching, exploratory music of his final years faster than most musicians manage one honest reinvention across a whole career.

What Veasley, Shaw, Faulk and Abdul Shaheed are doing instead is playing in his spirit rather than his script. Four musicians who already know each other's instincts get in a room, agree on a starting point, and then genuinely do not know where the tune ends up. No safety net, no arranged ending, nobody checking a chart to see what comes next. That is also, not coincidentally, the actual definition of jazz that a lot of programming quietly abandons the moment a crowd wants something familiar. This isn't even the first time this exact group has done a Coltrane Unscripted night in this room. An earlier date filled up well enough this year that we brought it back, which tells you people want the risk itself, not just a night of recognizable melodies.

## Four Musicians Who Already Made Their Own Case

Start with [Jaleel Shaw](https://www.jaleelshaw.com/bio), the alto saxophonist, because his story has a Philadelphia twist I love pointing out. He was born here in 1978 and came up through Philadelphia High School for the Creative and Performing Arts before Berklee and a master's degree at Manhattan School of Music. His 2005 debut album, Perspective, was good enough to catch Roy Haynes' attention, and Shaw spent the next fourteen years in Haynes' band, an apprenticeship with one of the last living links to the bebop era that most saxophonists would trade almost anything for. He teaches at his own alma mater in New York now and still comes home often enough that Philadelphia gets to claim him honestly, not just by accident of birth.

[Adam Faulk](https://thejazzsanctuary.com/adam-faulk/) came to the piano by a stranger route. He started on violin at five, switched to viola, and played that instrument with a leading youth orchestra in Greater Boston before Oberlin Conservatory redirected him toward jazz and toward keys. He served as musical director for the singer Billy Paul until Paul's death in 2016, which put him inside the machinery of Philadelphia soul at a level most transplants never reach, and he has lived and worked in this city long enough now that calling him a Boston export undersells how much of his sound is local.

[Khary Abdul Shaheed](https://www.allaboutjazz.com/musicians/khary-abdul-shaheed/) sits behind the drums and brings rhythm traditions that don't usually get anywhere near a Coltrane conversation, rhythms rooted in West Africa and in Cuba, played with the kind of tone and touch a small room actually lets you hear. That's part of what makes an unscripted set with him interesting. He isn't there to keep straightforward time under somebody else's idea. He's there to push the pulse somewhere Coltrane's own quartet never had a drummer positioned to go.

And then there is [Gerald Veasley](https://jazzphiladelphia.org/team/gerald-veasley/), the six string bassist who built the room this all happens in. He came up under Joe Zawinul and Grover Washington Jr., studied political science at the University of Pennsylvania, and has recorded ten albums as a leader, including one reimagining Charles Mingus and one paying tribute to Nina Simone. He is president of Jazz Philadelphia now, and in 2002 he founded his own Bass BootCamp to pass on what he learned to the next generation of players. He is also, not incidentally, the person who started the Unscripted Jazz Series in the first place.

## The City That Shaped Him First

None of this happens without Philadelphia's specific hold on Coltrane, so it is worth being precise about what that hold actually was, because I hear it flattened into one sentence more often than it deserves. He was born in Hamlet, North Carolina, in 1926 and grew up in High Point, where his family and his church shaped him early. He came to Philadelphia in June 1943, seventeen years old, fresh out of high school, and took a job at a sugar refinery. That September his mother bought him his first saxophone as a birthday gift, and from 1944 to 1945 he studied at the Ornstein School of Music before the Navy took him to Pearl Harbor for a year.

He came back to Philadelphia in 1946 and stayed close to this city's jazz scene for most of the next decade, [studying music theory under the guitarist Dennis Sandole and building the ear that would eventually produce Giant Steps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Coltrane). In 1952 he bought a rowhouse at 1511 North 33rd Street in Strawberry Mansion, and he kept ties to that house even after his career pulled him toward New York, living there off and on until 1958. His mother stayed there until 1977. [The property was named a National Historic Landmark in 1999](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Coltrane_House), and after decades of struggle and a real threat of demolition, the Coltrane family recovered ownership in 2024 and is now working with preservation partners on a restoration that is expected to open the house to the public. Ravi Coltrane, his son, has said he wants it to become a place with [performance, education and spiritual components](https://www.inquirer.com/news/philadelphia/john-coltrane-house-philadelphia-restoration-20251013.html), not a museum that only looks backward.

## A Room the Bynums Built, and One I Have Run for a Long Time

SOUTH sits at 600 North Broad Street in Spring Garden, and it opened in 2015 with a proposition I still think is harder to execute than it sounds: a Southern kitchen and a seventy five seat jazz parlor under one roof, with live music six nights a week, every week, not as a seasonal flourish but as the actual reason the room exists. [Robert and Benjamin Bynum](https://www.southjazzkitchen.com/our-story/) built it. They are thirty five year veterans of this city's hospitality world, and Benjamin runs the kitchen as Executive Chef. I have worked with this family for well over two decades now, going back to Zanzibar Blue, the room they built before this one, and I have managed SOUTH as general manager since the doors opened.

Gerald Veasley launched the Unscripted Jazz Series here in October 2015, working alongside his wife Roxanne, and the series has now presented [more than 140 nights of music](https://www.exploretock.com/south-unscripted-jazz-philadelphia/) in that room, drawing artists like Gerald Albright, Kirk Whalum, David Benoit and Randy Brecker through a space that seats fewer people than most high school classrooms hold. [Visit Philadelphia counts SOUTH among the city's essential jazz venues](https://www.visitphilly.com/articles/philadelphia/top-places-to-listen-to-live-jazz-music-in-philadelphia/), and I don't think that reputation happens by accident. It happens because a room decides, night after night for a decade, that the music gets the real commitment instead of the leftover attention.

## Landing in the Middle of His Own Centennial

Here is what makes this particular date land differently than it would in an ordinary year. John Coltrane was born September 23, 1926, which makes 2026 his centennial, and Philadelphia is not treating that quietly. [Coltrane 100 Philly](https://jazzphiladelphia.org/coltrane/), organized by Jazz Philadelphia alongside Creative PHL, WRTI and Ars Nova Workshop, runs [from July through November with more than two dozen concerts and community events](https://www.visitphilly.com/things-to-do/events/coltrane-100/) across the city, museum tours, a twenty four hour radio broadcast of his music, and a closing concert in November marking sixty years since his Temple University recording. Coltrane Unscripted at SOUTH sits inside that same season, thirteen days before what would have been his hundredth birthday.

I keep coming back to something I believe about this city and this year in general. Philadelphia's cultural life does not need a global spotlight to be real. It was real when Coltrane was riding the Broad Street subway to gigs in the 1940s, and it is real right now in the restoration crews working on that rowhouse in Strawberry Mansion, repairing windows and clearing a backyard that a jazz giant once walked through. A spotlight year can reveal what was already true. It cannot manufacture it. So when four musicians walk into our room on September 10 with no script and no safety net, chasing whatever idea the room gives them that night, they are not performing a tribute to something that happened a long time ago in a different city. They are doing the thing Coltrane actually did here, before anybody called him a legend. I will be there for both sets.
