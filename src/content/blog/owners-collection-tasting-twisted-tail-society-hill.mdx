THE BLUES, BABY. THE BLUES. I'm Harry Hayman, and on Thursday night I sat down at a long table in the back room of The Twisted Tail, at 509 S. 2nd Street in Society Hill, for our good friend George Reilly's Owner's Collection Tasting. George spent ten months collecting whiskey and about ten seconds reminding me why I love a good bar. Every place at that table had four small pours waiting on a paper mat, a glass of water beside them, a folded napkin and a candle burning down the middle of the runner. The bar kept it to 20 guests, which is exactly the right size for a night like this: big enough to feel like a party, small enough that the man pouring can tell you why each bottle is on the table. I came for the whiskey. I stayed for the vibe. This is the longer version of the night: the bottles, the grill, the music, and why I keep walking back to this block of Second Street.

## A Bourbon House and Juke Joint on Second Street

If you have not been, start with the address. The Twisted Tail sits on South 2nd Street by Headhouse Square, and the sign over the windows says it plainly: Bourbon House and Juke Joint. [Visit Philly's guide to Society Hill](https://www.visitphilly.com/areas/philadelphia-neighborhoods/society-hill/) lists it as a Southern inspired bourbon house and live music juke joint, in the same breath as the neighborhood's historic taverns and restaurants.

The block itself has history to spare. The [walking tour at USHistory.org](https://www.ushistory.org/tour/headhouse.htm) explains that in 1745 sheds went up along 2nd Street so merchants could gather and sell food and wares, with fire engine houses, the headhouses, at the ends of the sheds. [The Philadelphia History blog's account of Headhouse Market and the Shambles](https://philahistory.org/2021/02/05/the-history-of-philadelphias-headhouse-market-and-shambles/) adds that this was known for most of its history as the New Market, the first neighborhood offshoot of the old High Street Market. And Visit Philly notes that the Headhouse Square Farmers Market still sets up under the Shambles every Sunday. So a place built around food, drink and people gathering is right at home on this street. It has been that kind of street for almost three hundred years.

A word on the name. A [juke joint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juke_joint) is the African American term for an informal place with music, dancing and drinking that grew up across the southeastern United States. It is where a lot of the blues was played before anybody put it on a record. When a bar takes that name, I want the music to be real. At The Twisted Tail, it is.

## The Man Behind the Bottles

George knows his whiskey cold. Ask him about a bottle and you'll get the story behind it, why he wanted it, and what he's looking for when he takes that first sip. And the man clearly takes as much pleasure in sharing the stuff as he does in finding it. That's what made the Owner's Collection Tasting so damn good. The next morning I wrote to him and said what I meant: well done, very, very enjoyable, and a wealth of knowledge.

His path to that table is not the usual one. When [the Chestnut Hill Local profiled the bar in 2013](https://www.chestnuthilllocal.com/stories/not-twisted-any-more-chef-straightens-out-bluesy-pub,4404), it reported that George opened The Twisted Tail in July 2011 in the space Kildare's used to occupy, after a career as an actor that included Shakespearean roles. The same piece said he had trained at the Oxford School of Drama, had worked in pubs and managed bars in England, that his mother's side of the family was in the Scotch distillery business, and that he played blues guitar himself. Put those together and the room makes sense. A man who knows how to hold an audience, with Scotch distilling in the family and the blues in his hands, built a bar that does all three.

The invitation for this night said George had been collecting bottles for the past ten months with one evening in mind. On Thursday, November 19, at 6 o'clock, he opened them for a table of 20 at $195 a seat, a little before the Thanksgiving rush.

## Four Pours Worth the Wait

Old Rip Van Winkle 10 Year. Woodford Reserve Double Double Oak. Hibiki 21. Wild Turkey Austin Nichols Gold Foil. When Van Winkle has to fight for attention, George has put together one hell of a lineup. Here is a little about each, because every one of them has a story.

**Old Rip Van Winkle 10 Year.** The invitation called it a personal favorite of George's. [Buffalo Trace Distillery describes it](https://www.buffalotracedistillery.com/our-brands/van-winkle/old-rip-van-winkle-10-year/) as a wheated Kentucky straight bourbon bottled at 107 proof, nearly barrel strength, with only a touch of Kentucky limestone water after a decade of aging. The Van Winkle name carries a long tale of its own: according to [the history of the Van Winkle bourbons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pappy_Van_Winkle), Old Rip Van Winkle was introduced just before Prohibition and did not come back until after 1972, and today the family's bourbons are distilled and bottled at Buffalo Trace in Frankfort, Kentucky. People chase these bottles for years. George poured one for the table.

**Woodford Reserve Double Double Oaked.** This one is all about the barrel. [Woodford Reserve says](https://shop.woodfordreserve.com/double-double-oaked-7aqy/) it takes fully mature Double Oaked bourbon and finishes it for an additional two years in a second, heavily toasted, lightly charred new oak barrel, and bottles it at 90.4 proof. [Breaking Bourbon's review](https://www.breakingbourbon.com/review/woodford-reserve-double-double-oak-bourbon) notes it began in 2015 as a limited release in the distillery's own series and became a national limited edition in 2025. That night the Double Oaked and the Double Double Oaked stood side by side by the window, which is about the best lesson in what two extra years in a barrel can do.

**Hibiki 21.** The one pour from outside Kentucky. [Suntory describes Hibiki 21](https://house.suntory.com/hibiki-whisky/hibiki-21-years-old) as a blend of Japanese malt and grain whiskies aged for at least 21 years. [Hibiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hibiki_%28whisky%29) means resonance or echo, and the brand launched in 1989 with 17 and 21 year expressions. The invitation said this bottle was secured with a little help from the right connections, and I believe it.

**Wild Turkey Austin Nichols Gold Foil.** The invitation called it the bottle George couldn't wait to open and share, and you could see why. The name on the label goes back to the beginning of the brand. As [the history of Wild Turkey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wild_Turkey_%28bourbon%29) tells it, Austin Nichols was a wholesaler that bottled bourbon from the Ripy family's distillery near Lawrenceburg, Kentucky, and the Wild Turkey name stuck after an Austin Nichols executive, Thomas McCarthy, took some warehouse samples on a wild turkey hunting trip in 1940. His friends kept asking for that wild turkey bourbon. They're still asking.

## Get the Mushrooms. Get the Trout.

And can we talk about that grill? GET THE MUSHROOMS. GET THE TROUT. The mushrooms alone are worth a trip to Second Street.

The Twisted Tail cooks over charcoal, and the Chestnut Hill Local singled out its smoky charcoal grill items more than a decade ago. On Thursday the mushrooms came out dark and crisp at the edges, piled high and finished with fresh sprouts and a bright green herb sauce. The trout came skin side up over green beans, ringed with pink slices of radish, with a candle flickering next to the plate. There was also a board of small hand pies, toasts and skewered meatballs going around the table. Good whiskey needs good food next to it, and nobody at that table went hungry.

## The Blues, Baby

Then there's the reason I never want to leave this joint. THE BLUES. There's a particular kind of guitar note that makes you stop halfway through whatever you were saying. Let it hang there. Take a sip. Your story can wait.

Out by the bar a guitarist in black sat with his guitar and a small amp, playing the kind of blues that does not need to be loud to fill a room. The Chestnut Hill Local described the same thing in 2013: everyone loved the blues and the fun of the place. Thirteen years later that has not changed, and I hope it never does. A juke joint is supposed to have music in it, and this one does.

## A Room Like This Is Part of the City

I spend a lot of time thinking about hospitality, and I keep landing on the same idea: rooms like this are part of what holds a city together. A bar with an owner who still pours for the table, a grill that is worth the trip, live music on a weeknight, a street that has been a gathering place since the 1740s. None of that shows up in a budget line, but you feel it the minute you walk in.

George, you've got one of the coolest rooms in Philly. I came for the whiskey. I stayed for the vibe. If you go, sit where you can hear the guitar, order the mushrooms, and ask George about a bottle. Then let him tell you the story.
